# Intelligence Without a Mind

Intelligence is often recognised through what an organism can do. When an animal navigates a maze, we infer that it has processed information about its surroundings and adjusted its behaviour accordingly. _Physarum_ _polycephalum_, commonly known as a slime mould, is a single-celled organism that forms a spreading network of interconnected tubes rather than a body organised around a brain or nervous system. The tubes continually change in thickness, transporting nutrients through the organism as it grows across surfaces in search of food.<sup>1</sup> Despite lacking neurons, _Physarum_ can navigate mazes, reorganise its network in response to its surroundings, and form efficient connections between food sources. Its behaviour resembles problem-solving, although the biological processes producing it do not align with what we usually associate with thought.

In a 2000 study, Nakagaki et al. placed _Physarum_ in a maze with food sources positioned at its exits. The organism extended its network through the available passages, eventually retaining routes that connected the food sources and withdrawing from others.<sup>2</sup> No central structure compared possible routes or selected the shortest one. Instead, the organism's body changed as it explored, with growth and withdrawal shaping the network over time. 

A decade later, Tero et al. used _Physarum_ to model the Tokyo railway network, arranging food sources to represent urban centres and allowing the organism to develop connections between them. Its resulting network shared several features with the existing railway system, including efficient connections between important locations and a degree of redundancy.<sup>3</sup> The slime mould had not made the same decisions as human planners. Its network had emerged from simple rules of growth and adaptation, yet it shared some of the features of a human-designed railway system. But does producing an efficient result actually require intelligence?

Take a thermostat, for example. It responds to information from its surroundings, adjusting the temperature when conditions change, yet we would not ordinarily describe it as intelligent. Suppose it could retain information about previous temperatures and use that history to anticipate changes. It would now be able to adjust its responses based on past experience. If it could also learn from its actions and change its behaviour when its usual responses stopped working, would we consider it intelligent? Each new ability brings us closer to what we associate with intelligence, but it remains unclear where the boundary lies. How much does a system need to be able to do before we call it intelligent?

_Physarum_ also challenges our assumptions about how problem-solving works. We tend to imagine problem-solving as a process that takes place inside an organism before it acts on the world. A problem is represented, possible solutions are compared, and an action is selected. _Physarum_ appears to solve problems differently because its network is both the means by which it explores its surroundings and the structure through which its eventual solution emerges. Its body changes in response to the environment, and those changes alter what the organism can do next. There may be no clear boundary between processing information and acting on it. If intelligence can emerge through this continuous interaction, we may need to rethink our assumption that it requires a brain.

In 2008, Saigusa et al. reported that _Physarum_ altered its behaviour after repeated exposure to unfavourable conditions at regular intervals. When an expected interruption failed to occur, the organism's activity still reflected the timing of previous interruptions, although this response weakened over time when the interruptions stopped. The researchers interpreted these findings as evidence that the organism could anticipate periodic events based on its environmental history.<sup>4</sup> Earlier conditions had influenced how it behaved later, without a nervous system storing an account of what had happened. Calling this memory describes how past conditions shape subsequent behaviour, without implying that the organism consciously recalls an event.

Retaining information does not necessarily mean consciously recalling past events. A system's previous experiences can shape its present behaviour without us knowing how, or whether, it represents those experiences internally. In _Physarum_, previous conditions may leave traces in the organism's activity that influence what it does next. Whether such traces amount to memory in the same sense that we use the word for animals with nervous systems is a separate question. The behaviour provides evidence that the organism's history matters to its responses, but it does not settle what kind of process that history produces.

The thermostat thought experiment now carries more weight. A system that adjusts its responses according to past conditions has something more than a fixed reaction to a present stimulus, but it remains unclear whether this difference is enough to warrant the word intelligence. We might ask how much information the system retains, how flexibly it uses that information or how successfully it adapts when circumstances change. Each criterion places the boundary somewhere different. The same behaviour might count as intelligent under one definition and as a sophisticated form of regulation under another. A useful outcome does not establish that a system thinks, and complexity alone cannot settle the question.

Even if we agreed on which behaviours count as intelligent, another question would remain: does producing those behaviours tell us anything about what an organism experiences? Intelligence and consciousness are distinct problems. A system might process information, adapt to changing conditions, and solve problems without there being any evidence that it has a subjective experience of doing so. We infer consciousness in other people because we share their biology and they can describe their experiences to us. With other animals, their nervous systems, behaviour and evolutionary relationships with us give us further grounds for comparison. _Physarum_ sits outside these familiar points of reference. It has no brain, and its behaviour offers little insight into what, if anything, it experiences. We can study how it responds to its surroundings in considerable detail, but that knowledge does not tell us whether those responses are accompanied by subjective experience. An organism might meet a behavioural definition of intelligence without there being any evidence that it experiences the world as we do.

Perhaps the thermostat would need more than memory. Perhaps it would need to learn from its mistakes, change its strategy or apply what it has learned in circumstances it has never encountered. Yet each addition shifts the boundary rather than explaining exactly where it lies. A sufficiently elaborate machine could satisfy many of the criteria we associate with intelligence, while an organism such as _Physarum_ meets some of them through changes in the structure of its own body.

The difficulty may lie partly in the word itself. We group together capacities such as navigation, memory and adaptation under the heading of intelligence, even though a system can possess one without possessing the others. There may be no single threshold that separates intelligence from its absence, only different capacities that we decide are significant. If so, asking whether a slime mould is intelligent may tell us as much about the criteria we have chosen as it does about the organism itself.
