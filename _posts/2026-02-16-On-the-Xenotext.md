# On the Xenotext

Everything we write is vulnerable to time. Clay tablets fracture, paper rots in damp archives, and digital documents like the first draft of this essay can disappear when one's OneDrive fails to back up. Our methods of preserving knowledge have become increasingly sophisticated, yet they remain dependent on the continued existence of the materials, technologies, and institutions that sustain them. One attempt to rethink literary preservation comes from Canadian poet Christian Bök. His ongoing project, *The Xenotext*, uses the DNA of the radiation-resistant bacterium *Deinococcus radiodurans* as a medium for poetry. After approximately 25 years of work, Bök and his publisher announced the project’s completion in June 2025.

&nbsp;

In 1956, food microbiologist Arthur W. Anderson isolated a bacterium that had survived sterilisation experiments on canned ground meat. Initially designated *Micrococcus radiodurans*, researchers later reclassified the species as *Deinococcus radiodurans*, combining Greek and Latin roots to describe a formidable, radiation-resistant berry.<sup>1</sup> The organism withstands acute ionising radiation exposures of approximately 15,000 Gray, a threshold thousands of times higher than the lethal human dose. It exhibits comparable resistance to desiccation and extreme cold, prompting the moniker "Conan the Bacterium".

Ionising radiation shatters bacterial chromosomes into hundreds of fragments -- lethal to nearly all terrestrial life. *D. radiodurans* overcomes this damage through redundant genetic architecture, maintaining between 4 and 10 complete copies of its circular genome per cell. When radiation fractures these chromosomes, homologous sequences align across the fragments, serving as reciprocal templates during extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing. Through this multiphase enzymatic pathway, the cell reconstitutes an intact chromosome from fragments that appear entirely ruined. <sup>2</sup>

Multiple genomic copies make up only half of this protective mechanism. High-energy radiation generates copious reactive oxygen species capable of oxidising structural proteins and catalytic enzymes. Genomic reconstruction halts whenever oxidative stress destroys the DNA ligases and polymerases required for repair. Inside *D. radiodurans*, high concentrations of intracellular manganese complexes scavenge these reactive oxygen species, shielding critical repair proteins from carbonylation.<sup>3</sup> Radiotolerance rests on this dual conservation: safeguarding the genetic blueprint while preserving the catalytic machinery tasked with its reconstruction.

These cellular adaptations make *D. radiodurans* an exceptional candidate for long-term data storage. DNA packs information at molecular densities unmatched by synthetic silicon, while the host organism possesses enzymatic systems capable of maintaining genomic sequence integrity through severe environmental crises. Bök harnessed this biological resilience for an objective unrelated to bacterial fitness: the indefinite preservation of verse.

The project commenced with an original poem titled "Orpheus". Bök mapped the alphabetic text into a synthetic nucleotide sequence via a substitution cipher, assigning specific letters of the alphabet to corresponding triplets of nucleotides. Once synthesised, this artificial sequence was inserted into the host organism as an engineered plasmid construct.

The system functions as a reciprocal biochemical cipher. When cellular ribosomes transcribe and translate the "Orpheus" DNA sequence into an amino acid chain, the resulting protein decodes under a corresponding cipher key to reveal a companion poem, "Eurydice".<sup>4</sup> Operating within ribosomal translation rules imposed severe structural limits on the verse. Ribosomes process messenger RNA in three-nucleotide codons, each assigning one of twenty amino acids or dictating a stop command. Bök had to engineer an alphabetic sequence that simultaneously sustained lyrical coherence in English and satisfied the biochemical constraints of transcription and translation.

Theoretical compatibility with the genetic code fails to ensure biological success in vivo. Translated polypeptide chains must fold into stable secondary and tertiary structures without aggregating into toxic inclusion bodies or triggering rapid proteolysis by host enzymes. The engineered construct must replicate alongside endogenous genomic material without imposing an unsustainable metabolic burden on the host. Bök initially validated the reciprocal cipher in *Escherichia coli*, demonstrating that the synthetic gene expressed the expected protein without killing the cell. Transitioning the system into *D. radiodurans* presented harsher biochemical barriers, requiring the construct to remain stable within an extremophile whose complex repair machinery continuously scrutinises foreign DNA.

Natural selection preserves the endogenous genome of *D. radiodurans* by eliminating mutations that impair essential enzymes or cellular integrity. The synthetic "Orpheus" sequence operates under different evolutionary pressures. Because the inserted gene provides no advantage for bacterial survival or radiation resistance, point mutations within the construct carry negligible fitness costs for the host. The bacterium's DNA repair pathways protect against environmental trauma, but routine replication errors and genetic drift accumulate over thousands of generations. Over evolutionary timescales, these unselected mutations degrade the cipher, altering the encoded letters.

I first encountered *The Xenotext* as an undergraduate while writing an essay on *Deinococcus*. At the time, the project seemed primarily an engineering feat: translating text into codons and embedding it within a resilient organism. Six years later, the central interest lies in the distinction between biochemical activity and literary interpretation. Inside the cell, the sequence operates as a template for RNA polymerase and ribosomes, generating a physical polypeptide. For human readers, the same sequence functions as verse, its lines shaped by mythic allusion and poetic form. The bacterium executes these biochemical instructions without access to the language or literary intentions embedded in the cipher.

Bök conceived *The Xenotext* as a work capable of outlasting both its author and human civilisation. 6 In this speculative scenario, the bacterial lineage survives across millions of years, carrying the insert long after human extinction. An observer equipped with sequencing technology could isolate the nucleotide chain and reverse the substitution cipher. Reconstructing the alphabetic text falls short of recovering the poem. Deciphered letters provide data, but reading poetry requires familiarity with the English language and the myth of Orpheus. Without these cultural reference points, the extracted string appears as an arbitrary code rather than a poem.

One counterargument suggests that deep-time survival beyond human existence misses the true achievement of Bök's work. The poem already exists in print, circulating among living readers, which allows the bacterial genome to serve as an enduring biological backup. The bacterium also actively participates in the artistic system. Rather than storing dormant code, the living cell transcribes the "Orpheus" sequence and translates it into a protein that decodes as "Eurydice". In a biochemical sense, the organism reads the synthetic gene and writes a companion poem in return.

*D. radiodurans* achieves what no archive of paper or stone can match: it integrates poetic text into a living metabolic cycle. This biological dialogue remains complete only while human readers exist to interpret both sides of the cipher. Once human language vanishes, genetic drift will slowly degrade the insert, and the cell will continue expressing a mutating protein without an audience to recognise the verse. *The Xenotext* ultimately creates less of an eternal monument than a temporary, living collaboration between human literary form and bacterial biology.
