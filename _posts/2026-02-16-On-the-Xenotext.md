# On the Xenotext

Everything we write is vulnerable to time. Clay tablets fracture, paper rots in damp archives, and digital documents like the first draft of this essay can disappear with a failed hard drive. Our methods of preserving knowledge have become increasingly sophisticated, yet they remain dependent on the continued existence of the materials, technologies, and institutions that sustain them. One attempt to rethink literary preservation comes from Canadian poet Christian Bök. His ongoing project, *The Xenotext*, uses the DNA of the radiation-resistant bacterium *Deinococcus radiodurans* as a medium for poetry. After approximately 25 years of work, Bök and his publisher announced the project’s completion in June 2025.

&nbsp;

In 1956, food microbiologist Arthur W. Anderson isolated a bacterium that had survived sterilisation experiments on canned ground meat. Initially designated *Micrococcus radiodurans*, researchers later reclassified the species as *Deinococcus radiodurans*, combining Greek and Latin roots to describe a formidable, radiation-resistant berry.<sup>1</sup> The organism withstands acute ionising radiation exposures of approximately 15,000 Gray, a threshold thousands of times higher than the lethal human dose. It exhibits comparable resistance to desiccation and extreme cold, prompting the moniker "Conan the Bacterium".

Ionising radiation shatters bacterial chromosomes into hundreds of fragments -- lethal to nearly all terrestrial life. *D. radiodurans* overcomes this damage through redundant genetic architecture, maintaining between 4 and 10 complete copies of its circular genome per cell. When radiation fractures these chromosomes, homologous sequences align across the fragments, serving as reciprocal templates during extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing. Through this multiphase enzymatic pathway, the cell reconstitutes an intact chromosome from fragments that appear entirely ruined.<sup>3</sup>

Multiple genomic copies make up only half of this protective mechanism. High-energy radiation generates copious reactive oxygen species capable of oxidising structural proteins and catalytic enzymes. Genomic reconstruction stops whenever oxidative stress destroys the DNA ligases and polymerases required for repair. Inside *D. radiodurans*, high concentrations of intracellular manganese complexes scavenge these reactive oxygen species, shielding critical repair proteins from carbonylation. <sup>4</sup> Radiotolerance rests on this dual conservation: safeguarding the genetic blueprint while preserving the catalytic machinery tasked with its reconstruction.

These cellular adaptations make *D. radiodurans* an exceptional candidate for long-term data storage. DNA packs information at molecular densities unmatched by synthetic silicon, while the host organism possesses enzymatic systems capable of maintaining genomic sequence integrity through severe environmental crises. Bök harnessed this biological resilience for an objective unrelated to bacterial fitness: the indefinite preservation of verse.

&nbsp;

The project commenced with an original poem titled "Orpheus". Bök mapped the alphabetic text into a synthetic nucleotide sequence via a substitution cipher, assigning specific letters of the alphabet to corresponding triplets of nucleotides. Once synthesised, this artificial sequence was inserted into the host organism as an engineered plasmid construct.

The system functions as a reciprocal biochemical cipher. When cellular ribosomes transcribe and translate the *Orpheus* DNA sequence into an amino acid chain, the resulting protein decodes under a corresponding cipher key to reveal a companion poem, *Eurydice*.  <sup>5</sup> Operating within ribosomal translation rules imposed severe structural limits on the verse. Ribosomes process messenger RNA in three-nucleotide codons, each assigning one of twenty amino acids or dictating a stop command. Bök had to engineer an alphabetic sequence that simultaneously sustained lyrical coherence in English and satisfied the biochemical constraints of transcription and translation.

Theoretical compatibility with the genetic code fails to ensure biological success in vivo. Translated polypeptide chains must fold into stable secondary and tertiary structures without aggregating into toxic inclusion bodies or triggering rapid proteolysis by host enzymes. The engineered construct must replicate alongside endogenous genomic material without imposing an unsustainable metabolic burden on the host. Bök initially validated the reciprocal cipher in *Escherichia coli*, demonstrating that the synthetic gene expressed the expected protein without killing the cell. Transitioning the system into *D. radiodurans* presented harsher biochemical barriers, requiring the construct to remain stable within an extremophile whose complex repair machinery continuously scrutinises foreign DNA.

The project's completion in 2025 joined two fundamentally disparate semiotic structures. Cellular machinery interprets genetic code strictly through stereochemical interactions and enzymatic affinities. Human readers process poetic form through historical linguistic conventions and interpretive literacy. The bacterium expresses the synthetic sequence as a physical chain of amino acids; the status of that sequence as poetry exists solely within human consciousness.

&nbsp;

Natural selection preserves the native genome of *D. radiodurans* through rigorous evolutionary pressure. Nucleotide substitutions or deletions that disable critical metabolic enzymes or compromise structural integrity reduce organismal fitness, ensuring that purifying selection eliminates deleterious mutations across generations. The bacterium's formidable repair apparatus operates in service of this biological continuity.

The synthetic *Orpheus* insert occupies a neutral niche within this evolutionary dynamic. Because the encoded verse does not contribute to any metabolic efficiency or stress tolerance, point mutations within the synthetic construct carry negligible fitness costs for the host. The bacterial cell processes the artificial construct strictly as chemical substrate, treating poetic strophes identically to non-coding genetic sequences. Evolutionary forces drift freely across non-essential sequences. Although enzymatic repair pathways shield the chromosome from acute environmental trauma, long-term genomic fidelity remains subject to replication errors and genetic drift over thousands of generations. Physical durability of the bacterial host enables nucleotide retention, while unconstrained genetic drift gradually alters the synthetic cipher over deep evolutionary timescales.

I first encountered *The Xenotext* as a first-year undergraduate, when I authored an essay about the possibility of preserving literature in bacterial DNA. At the time, I was primarily interested in the technical achievement. Could a poem be encoded in DNA? Could a living organism preserve it? The appeal lay in the possibility of using biology as an unconventional archive. Six years later, I am more interested in the asymmetry between what the organism preserves and what the poet hopes to preserve. For the bacterium, the sequence is a physical arrangement of nucleotides, but for Bök, it is a poem, carrying a meaning that extends beyond its molecular composition.

A single macromolecule inhabits two incommensurable domains of value. Within the cytoplasm, its value operates through replication fidelity and transcriptive expression. Within human culture, value arises from linguistic literacy and interpretive hermeneutics. The physical sequence endures in the bacterium entirely divorced from the semantic world of human language; the poetry exists solely in the minds capable of deciphering Bök's deliberate literary construct.

&nbsp;

Bök conceived *The Xenotext* as an artefact capable of outlasting both its creator and human civilisation. 6 In this speculative timeline, the engineered bacterial lineage propagates across millions of years, maintaining the synthetic sequence long after human extinction. An extraterrestrial or post-human intelligence possessing advanced sequencers and knowledge of the universal genetic code could conceivably extract the nucleotide string and invert the cipher. The cultural matrix that produced "Orpheus", conversely, would remain extinct. The myth of the Thracian poet and the historical circumstances of the poem's composition would be absent from the recovered data.

Traditional books remain dormant in physical libraries across centuries until literate readers reconstruct their meaning. Even across millennia, philologists decipher ancient scrolls by triangulating surviving lexicons and archaeological records. In the deep future imagined by Bök, those contextual scaffolding systems vanish entirely. A hypothetical decipherer might reconstruct the alphabetic string of "Orpheus" as pure data, mistaking a crafted lyrical work for an arbitrary cryptographic message.

Archival preservation unites physical durability with interpretive continuity. *The Xenotext* divorces these two requirements by consigning literary text to an autonomous biological carrier. *D. radiodurans* replicates the synthetic strand across countless cell divisions, functioning as an unwitting custodian of an uncomprehended message.

The chemical sequence persists in perpetuity, while the hermeneutic horizon necessary to illuminate it dissolves. An observer can recover the exact sequence of letters from the microbial chromosome even if every human language has vanished into deep time. The ultimate legacy of Bök's experiment hinges on where literature truly resides: within the physical stability of an encoded sequence, or within the active consciousness of an interpreting reader. If verse requires a living mind capable of aesthetic recognition, then a silent gene replicating inside *Deinococcus radiodurans* is only an elaborate chemical trace, preserving information while the poem itself has ceased to exist.
