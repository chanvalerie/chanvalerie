## Congratulations on the Exchange Rate!

When I was choosing where to study abroad, I looked at universities in several countries and eventually chose the UK partly because the fees seemed relatively cheap. As an international student, I was looking at tuition fees of around £30,000 a year and thought that was a good deal compared with some of my other options. This is *absolutely* a ridiculous thing to say because £30,000 is an enormous amount of money to most people, but I was comparing it with what I could have paid elsewhere, so the number had acquired a meaning that had very little to do with the number itself.

Visualise this another way. A home student in England can currently pay a maximum of £9,790 a year in undergraduate tuition, while a student at the University of South California pays $75,384 a year.<sup>1</sup> An American student looking at those figures could very reasonably think that English university is dirt cheap. Someone in England who has spent years worrying about the cost of their degree and paying off their student loans might find that description almost insulting. The same price can mean very different things depending on who is paying it.

I think about this whenever I hear European tourists in Southeast Asia say that everything is so cheap. A £3 meal, a taxi for a few pounds or a hotel room for £50 can feel astonishingly inexpensive when you are used to paying several times that at home. I understand the feeling because I experience it myself whenever I come back to Indonesia. After a few days of watching your money stretch further than it normally does, it becomes easy to stop thinking about the comparison and start thinking of the place itself as cheap.

For many Southeast Asians, those prices are the economic reality they were born into. They reflect the wages available to them, the opportunities around them and, in some places, a result of decades of poor political and economic decisions by their governments. Being told that your country is wonderfully cheap can sound very different when you are the person living with those economic realities.

Imagine how strange it would feel if a wealthy Saudi arrived in England and kept saying, “London is so cheap! £2000 a month for a studio is peanuts!” We’d probably think they were being a bit of a twat. We would understand that they weren’t really describing England as it feels to the people who live there; they were describing what England feels like to them, with their income, their currency and their ability to spend without worrying about every purchase. Yet when tourists say the same thing about Southeast Asian countries, we often accept it as an objective description rather than recognising it as a statement about their own financial position.

A tourist can sit in a restaurant, pay a few pounds for a meal and feel as though they have found an incredible bargain, while the person cooking it may earn a wage that makes eating there themselves a rare occasion. The price is tiny relative to one person’s income and significant relative to another’s, even though they are both looking at the same menu. The tourist isn’t necessarily doing anything wrong by enjoying the meal, but the difference in what that price represents is, in my opinion, worth remembering.

The phrase “it’s so cheap here” irritates me more than I think it should. I know what people usually mean by it. They are surprised that their money goes further than it does at home, and I understand that feeling because I have experienced exactly the same thing in reverse. I think what bothers me is how easily I can take the feeling that my money goes further somewhere and turn it into a description of the place itself.

Don’t get me wrong, tourism absolutely brings value to the area. Foreign visitors bring money into local economies, support businesses, and provide employment, particularly in places that depend heavily on tourism. In Indonesia, tourism accounted for 4.7% of GDP and 17.5% of employment in tourism-related industries in 2023.<sup>2</sup> The fact that a holiday can be good value for someone arriving with pounds or euros is part of what makes these destinations attractive in the first place.

Spending can also influence what businesses choose to build and who they choose to serve. A restaurant aimed at foreign tourists can charge prices that are affordable to someone earning in pounds or euros but far removed from what a local worker would normally spend on a meal. Accommodation can be marketed towards people with foreign incomes because they can pay more. In places where tourism becomes a major source of income, residents can end up living alongside an economy increasingly calibrated to the purchasing power of visitors.

I’m not immune to the appeal of it myself — I absolutely relish the fact that my money stretches further in Indonesia than it does in England. There’s nothing inherently wrong with enjoying this fact, but I suppose what I wish I saw more of was a little more awareness of how we talk about it: who we’re saying it to, and the context in which we’re saying it. “My money goes surprisingly far here” is a statement about your own circumstances, while “everything here is so cheap” can come across very differently when you’re saying it to someone who lives here, earns here, and can’t experience the same prices with a foreign salary.

We rarely say, “My money goes much further in Indonesia”; we say, “Indonesia is cheap.” An observation about our own purchasing power becomes a description of someone else’s country, as if the cheapness belongs to Indonesia rather than to the relationship between the price and the person paying it. I suspect I’ll keep catching myself on the wording. “My money goes further here” is probably a much more honest sentence.
