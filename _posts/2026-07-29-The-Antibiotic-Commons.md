## The Antibiotic Commons

In the summer of 1928, Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming left his laboratory bench for a 14-day holiday. Upon returning, he found *Penicillium notatum* growing in an uncovered culture plate, surrounded by a clear ring where bacterial growth had been inhibited.<sup>1</sup> The mould was producing a compound that killed *Staphylococcus*, and Fleming named it penicillin. It took Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and their colleagues at Oxford more than a decade to purify it and test it as a drug. Fleming himself saw the danger early: in his Nobel lecture in 1945 he warned that careless use of penicillin could teach bacteria to resist it. Antibiotic resistance is now estimated to have directly caused about 1.27 million deaths in 2019 and to have contributed to nearly five million.<sup>2</sup>

Conventional commons take visible, physical forms like pastures or coastal fisheries, and its users can see when it is running low. Antibiotic effectiveness operates as an invisible aggregate probability. Every use of an antimicrobial agent alters selective pressures within microbial ecosystems, favouring resistant strains and lowering the statistical likelihood that future treatments will cure infection. Elinor Ostrom found that communities manage shared resources well when members can monitor use and sanction overuse. Healthcare, however, has no equivalent of either.<sup>3</sup> Prescribers are numerous and independent; each decision is made in a consulting room; its biological cost spreads across distant populations over decades. The clinician who signs the prescription passes the long-term cost of resistance to patients who do not yet exist.

Every antibiotic course kills susceptible bacteria and leaves resistant variants free to multiply.<sup>4</sup> Across billions of treatments this pressure accumulates and steadily pushes up baseline resistance, with no gauge to show how much remains and no moment at which the supply runs out. Efficacy fades unevenly: a drug can clear infections in one hospital ward and fail across a neighbouring region. The global loss is the sum of many local evolutionary shifts, each too small to notice from inside any one clinic.

Stewardship programmes try to protect this shared effectiveness but struggle to do so consistently. I don't think the cause is a shortage of knowledge, since most clinicians understand the data on resistance, and immediate patient demands and liability concerns tend to outweigh statistical abstractions. Educational campaigns treat overprescribing as an information gap. I see it primarily as a structural trap built on asymmetric clinical risks. Consider a physician seeing a patient with acute respiratory symptoms of unclear cause. Withholding antibiotics exposes the physician to liability if the illness worsens into bacterial sepsis, and prescribing an unnecessary broad-spectrum drug removes that uncertainty at no immediate cost to the prescriber. The resulting selection pressure is invisible in the clinic, and its consequences fall on future patients nobody can identify. Asking individuals for restraint under these rules asks clinicians to absorb acute liability in exchange for a delayed, anonymous public good, and voluntary appeals tend to give way against such defensive pressures.

Clinical debates tend to overshadow agriculture, where veterinary medicine uses enormous volumes of antibiotics on herds and flocks. One widely cited estimate puts the share of antimicrobials sold worldwide that go to food animals at 73%.<sup>5</sup> In many countries antibiotics are still given in low doses to promote growth and to offset crowded housing, although the European Union banned the practice in 2006 and the United States ended it for medically important drugs in 2017. Resistant strains that emerge on farms reach people through runoff, direct contact and the food supply. Because urban populations deal with clinics and rarely see feedlots, public attention settles on prescriptions and overlooks the comparable selective pressure generated across the food system.

The market compounds the problem at the development stage. Public health authorities want a new antibiotic held back on hospital shelves and used only against pathogens that resist everything else, and a company that has spent hundreds of millions of dollars on trials needs sales volume to recover that investment. Conservation and revenue pull in opposite directions. Large pharmaceutical companies have closed or shrunk their anti-infective divisions over the past two decades, and the small biotechnology firms that do bring a new drug to approval can still fail: Achaogen filed for bankruptcy in 2019, less than a year after its antibiotic plazomicin was approved, and Melinta followed later that year.<sup>6</sup> Better bedside prescribing cannot repair a collapsing pipeline unless the economics of drug development are restructured alongside it.

Most public health successes align collective welfare with personal protection. A person who is immunised gains direct protection against the disease, and a driver who fastens a seatbelt is protected in the next collision, so adherence rests on self-interest. Antibiotic conservation offers the individual patient only a modest return, in the form of fewer side effects and a lower risk of complications such as *Clostridioides difficile* infection. Most of the benefit goes to people who will never know they received it. A patient who declines antibiotics for a self-limiting respiratory infection puts up with fever and missed work today, and the preserved efficacy belongs to anonymous future populations. Patients and providers are asked to absorb immediate discomfort to protect a commons they will never see.

Antimicrobial conservation is at bottom a question of intergenerational justice -- about what we owe to generations that do not yet exist. Philosophical work on intergenerational equity usually concerns atmospheric carbon, but the depletion of antibiotic efficacy poses the same dilemma. An infant born thirty years from now will enter a microbial landscape shaped by prescriptions written today, and will inherit fewer treatment options from decisions made without their consent or voice. New technologies such as rapid molecular diagnostics and novel antimicrobial platforms might alter that path, and governance still has to manage resources under deep uncertainty. A patient cured of sepsis in 2060 will never trace their survival to a prescription that went unwritten in 2026. Stewardship exists to keep medical choices open for successors who cannot speak for themselves.

Education and clinical guidelines remain important in clear-cut cases and can curb flagrant misuse. They leave the underlying drivers in place: a family physician still weighs a concrete risk of malpractice claims against statistics about distant populations, and developers still lack a financial model for a drug meant to be used sparingly. Current market structures penalise the restraint that epidemiology calls for. Treating antibiotic efficacy as an invisible commons therefore calls for structural intervention in place of appeals to individual virtue. One option is delinked payment, in which a health system pays a fixed subscription for access to a new antibiotic regardless of how much is used, as the UK's NHS has begun to do. Another is legal protection for clinicians who withhold treatment that is not clearly indicated. Both bring the incentives of prescribers and developers into line with the interests of future patients, and preserving modern medicine's foundational therapies depends on that alignment.
