# On the Xenotext

Everything we write is vulnerable to time. Clay tablets fracture, paper decays, and digital documents like the first draft of this essay can disappear with a failed hard drive. Our methods of preserving knowledge have become increasingly sophisticated, yet they remain dependent on the continued existence of the materials, technologies, and institutions that sustain them. One attempt to rethink literary preservation comes from Canadian poet Christian Bök. His ongoing project, The Xenotext, uses the DNA of the radiation-resistant bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans as a medium for poetry. And after approximately 25 years of work, Bök and his publisher announced the project’s completion in June 2025.

---

&nbsp;

In 1956, food microbiologist Arthur W. Anderson discovered that a bacterium had survived radiation treatment intended to sterilise a tin of ground meat. Initially classified as Micrococcus radiodurans, the organism was later reclassified as Deinococcus radiodurans, a name derived from Greek and Latin roots that roughly translate to a radiation-resistant, formidable berry. <sup>1</sup>
The name is well earned: D. radiodurans can survive acute ionising radiation doses of approximately 15,000 Gy, thousands of times the dose that would be lethal to a human. It also tolerates severe dehydration and other environmental stresses, earning it the nickname “Conan the Bacterium”. <sup>2</sup>

Radiation still causes extensive damage to the organism; ionising radiation can fragment its DNA into hundreds of pieces, disrupting the genome in ways that would be fatal to many other organisms. D. radiodurans survives partly because it carries multiple copies of its genome. Overlapping fragments from these copies provide templates that the cell can use to reconstruct damaged DNA. Through coordinated repair processes, including extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing, the bacterium can gradually reassemble a functional genome from fragments that initially appear irreparable. <sup>3</sup>

For years, the presence of multiple genome copies was considered the central explanation for this resistance. However, repairing DNA requires more than intact templates. Radiation also generates reactive oxygen species that can damage proteins, including the enzymes responsible for DNA repair. Even with sufficient genetic material available, a cell cannot rebuild its genome if the machinery required to do is non-functional.

In D. radiodurans, high intracellular manganese concentrations help protect proteins against oxidative damage, allowing much of the cellular machinery to remain functional after irradiation. <sup>4</sup> Its survival therefore depends on protecting both its genetic material and the proteins needed to restore it.

For these reasons, D. radiodurans make the ideal choice for Bök’s project. DNA is already an exceptionally effective medium for storing biological information, and D. radiodurans has evolved mechanisms that allow it to preserve and recover that information under extreme conditions. Bök’s ambition was to use this durability for a purpose far removed from the organism’s own survival: the preservation of a poem.

---

&nbsp;

Bök began with a poem called “Orpheus”, which he translated into a synthetic DNA sequence using a custom cipher that assigns letters to codons, the three-nucleotide units through which genetic information is interpreted. The sequence could then be incorporated into a bacterial genome as an engineered genetic construct.

The concept extended beyond simply storing a poem in DNA: Bök designed the sequence so that when the bacterium expressed it, the resulting protein could be decoded through a second part of his system to recover another poem, “Eurydice”. The intended arrangement was reciprocal: one poem encoded in DNA, another recoverable from the information expressed through the genetic system. <sup>5</sup> The challenge was to encode a poem in a system governed by its own biological rules. During translation, the ribosome reads mRNA in groups of three nucleotides called codons, each of which specifies an amino acid or signals the end of translation. Bök therefore had to find a sequence that could carry literary meaning while still functioning within the constraints of the genetic code.

Even a sequence that satisfies these requirements may fail biologically. The amino acid chain it encodes must fold into a suitable structure, and its stability depends on the chemical properties of the resulting protein. The engineered sequence must also function within its host, where its expression and effects are shaped by the bacterium’s existing molecular machinery. The initial proof of concept was demonstrated in Escherichia coli. Establishing the system in D. radiodurans was a more demanding challenge, requiring the literary encoding to operate within an organism chosen for its exceptional capacity to withstand environmental damage.

The project’s completion, announced in 2025, marked the culmination of a long attempt to bring together two systems of information. The genetic code is interpreted through biochemical processes within the cell; the literary code depends on language, convention and the capacity of a reader to make meaning from words. The bacterium can translate the sequence into a protein. Whether that sequence also constitutes a poem is a different question, one that depends on the human ability to recognise and interpret it as language.

---

&nbsp;

The genome of D. radiodurans contains the information required for its growth and reproduction. Mutations that disrupt essential functions can reduce its ability to survive, creating opportunities for natural selection to favour variants that preserve those functions more effectively. The organism’s DNA repair mechanisms are part of this broader biological relationship between genetic information and survival.

The Orpheus sequence occupies a different position. Its literary meaning is irrelevant to the bacterium’s immediate needs. If the sequence changes without affecting the organism’s fitness, natural selection may have little reason to eliminate the mutation. The cell does not recognise the words encoded by the sequence, nor does it distinguish between a passage of literature and any other stretch of DNA. Preserving the sequence, however, does not necessarily mean preserving what it represents. The bacterium can maintain a sequence because it remains physically present and can continue to be replicated. Its ability to repair DNA may help protect that sequence from damage, although long-term persistence also depends on mutation, selection and the stability of the engineered construct. Biological durability does not guarantee that every nucleotide will remain unchanged indefinitely.

I first encountered The Xenotext as a first-year undergraduate, when I wrote an essay about the possibility of preserving literature in bacterial DNA. At the time, I was primarily interested in the technical achievement. Could a poem be encoded in DNA? Could a living organism preserve it? The appeal lay in the possibility of using biology as an unconventional archive. Six years later, I am more interested in the asymmetry between what the organism preserves and what the poet hopes to preserve. For the bacterium, the sequence is a physical arrangement of nucleotides, but for Bök, it is a poem, carrying a meaning that extends beyond its molecular composition.

The same DNA can therefore belong to two very different systems of value. Within the cell, its significance is determined by the processes through which it is replicated and expressed. Within human culture, its significance depends on a shared understanding of language, literary form, and the intentions of the person who wrote it. The sequence can persist in the bacterium without its literary meaning ever becoming part of the organism’s world -- that meaning belongs to the people who can read the poem and understand what Bök intended it to say.

---

&nbsp;

Bök has described The Xenotext as an attempt to create a work capable of surviving its author, civilisation, and potentially humanity itself. <sup>6</sup> Imagine the engineered sequence persisting for millions of years, passed between bacterial generations long after human civilisation has disappeared. The DNA might remain intact enough to be recovered, and its encoded information might still be decipherable by an intelligence with sufficient knowledge of the genetic code and Bök’s cipher. The cultural world in which “Orpheus” was written, however, might be entirely lost. The language, literary traditions and references that shaped the poem would no longer necessarily be available to whoever found it.

Books can go unread for centuries and still be recovered by people who know how to interpret them. Even when the author is long dead, surviving languages, historical records and other works can help a reader understand the world in which a text was written. In the future Bök imagines, those connections might no longer exist. Someone could decipher the sequence and recover the words of “Orpheus” without knowing that they formed a poem, let alone understanding the circumstances in which it was written.

We preserve books and archives in the hope that their contents will remain accessible to people in the future. Their physical survival is one part of that process; the knowledge needed to interpret them is another. The Xenotext brings these two conditions apart by placing a literary work in a medium that can replicate itself without any awareness of what it carries. The bacterium can maintain the sequence through successive generations, but nothing in its biology requires the poem to be recognised or understood.

The sequence might therefore persist long after the context needed to interpret it has disappeared. Its words could remain recoverable even if the language and literary traditions through which they acquired meaning were lost. Whether this would count as the survival of the poem depends on how much of a literary work we locate in the information it contains, and how much in the possibility of someone interpreting it.
