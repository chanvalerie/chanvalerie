# The Poet Who Wrote Inside a Bacterium

Everything we have ever written is, eventually, temporary. Clay tablets crumble, paper rots, and my OneDrive occasionally decides that a document I worked on for three hours no longer exists (case in point: the first draft of this essay). A Canadian poet called Christian Bök decided that if writing was going to have any chance of lasting, he needed to put it somewhere much better at surviving than we are. Enter *Deinococcus radiodurans*.


*Deinococcus radiodurans* is an extremophilic bacterium famously difficult to kill. Bök spent around twenty-five years trying to preserve a poem inside its genome in his project, *The Xenotext*. In June 2025, Bök and his publisher announced that the project was complete.

This was the subject of the very first essay I wrote as a lowly microbiology undergrad, and for some reason, *D. radiodurans* has remained one of those pieces of science I keep coming back to. Some might say it's my microbiological Roman Empire.

### Conan the bacterium

In 1956, a food microbiologist named Arthur W. Anderson subjected a tin of ground meat to radiation doses that were supposed to sterilise it. Something survived anyway, and the surviving organism was initially classified as *Micrococcus radiodurans* before being reclassified as *Deinococcus radiodurans*, a name that roughly translates to "terrible berry that withstands radiation".

The name is not an exaggeration: *D. radiodurans* can survive acute ionising radiation doses of around 15,000 Gy, thousands of times the dose that would be fatal to a human. It can also tolerate severe dehydration and other environmental stresses, which has earned it the nickname “Conan the Bacterium”.

Radiation still damages it -- ionising radiation can still break its DNA into hundreds of fragments, leaving the genome in pieces that would be catastrophic for most organisms. What makes *D. radiodurans* unusual is that it carries multiple copies of its genome, allowing overlapping fragments from different copies to act as templates for reconstructing the sequence. Through a series of repair processes, including extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing, the cell can gradually rebuild a genome that initially looked beyond repair.

For a long time, having multiple copies of its genome seemed like the obvious explanation for its resistance. But radiation does not only damage DNA; it also generates reactive oxygen species that can oxidise the proteins responsible for repairing that DNA. A cell could therefore have plenty of DNA fragments available as templates and still die if the molecular machinery needed to put them back together had already been destroyed. It turns out that *D. radiodurans* accumulates unusually high concentrations of manganese, which helps protect proteins from oxidative damage. The bacterium therefore survives radiation by protecting the machinery needed to repair its DNA as well as the DNA itself. 

This is what makes the organism such an interesting choice for Bök's project. DNA is already an information-storage system, and *D. radiodurans* is unusually good at protecting and repairing its own. Bök, however, wants it to preserve something that has absolutely nothing to do with the bacterium's survival, which is not exactly what evolution has trained bacteria to do.

### Putting a poem into DNA

The premise of *The Xenotext* is surprisingly straightforward. Bök wrote a short poem called "Orpheus" and converted it into a sequence of synthetic DNA using a custom cipher that assigns letters to DNA codons. That sequence could then be inserted into a bacterium and treated as a gene.

The clever part is what happens next. When the bacterium transcribes and translates the sequence, it produces a protein. Bök designed the DNA sequence so that the resulting protein could itself be decoded through another part of his system to produce a second poem, "Eurydice". The bacterium therefore carries one poem in its DNA and produces the information needed to recover another.

Making that work is considerably harder than the basic idea makes it sound. DNA is read in three-base units called codons, with each codon normally corresponding to an amino acid. Some codons instead tell the cell to stop translating. Bök therefore had to construct a literary cipher that also obeyed the rules of the genetic code, because a sequence that made perfect sense as English could easily produce a protein that stopped halfway through.

There was another problem at the other end. A DNA sequence does not become a meaningful protein simply because it has been designed to encode one. The resulting amino acid chain still has to fold into a stable structure, and that depends on the chemistry of the protein rather than the literary meaning of the sequence. The gene also has to work inside the organism carrying it, which introduces another set of biological constraints.

The first proof of concept was demonstrated in *E. coli*, but the real challenge was getting the system to work in *D. radiodurans*, the organism that made the whole project interesting in the first place. After years of work, Bök announced in 2025 that the project had finally been completed and that the engineered bacterium could produce the intended response.

There is a caveat worth making here. The final claim comes from Bök and his publisher, in the context of the publication of *The Xenotext: Book 2*, rather than from an independent peer-reviewed paper that has reproduced the result. That does not mean the experiment did not work, but it does mean the completion of the project currently rests on a different kind of evidence from the underlying biology.

Even if we take the result at face value, however, there is a much bigger problem.

### What does the bacterium actually preserve?

*D. radiodurans* is extraordinarily good at repairing its genome because its genome matters to it. The information encoded in its DNA determines how the cell functions, so mutations that damage essential genes can prevent it from surviving or reproducing. There is therefore an evolutionary reason for the bacterium to preserve those sequences.

A poem does not have the same advantage.

Once inserted into the genome, the Orpheus sequence is foreign genetic material. If it mutates without affecting the bacterium's ability to survive, there may be no reason for natural selection to remove that mutation. The cell does not know that a particular sequence represents a poem. It has no concept of English, authorship or literary meaning. As far as the bacterium is concerned, the sequence is simply DNA.

This creates an odd limitation to the whole idea. Bök is borrowing a form of biological durability that evolved to preserve information because that information is useful to the organism. The poem does not become useful simply because we consider it meaningful. Its survival inside the bacterium is therefore not guaranteed by the very property that makes *D. radiodurans* so resilient.

This is the part of the project I find much more interesting now than I did when I first encountered it. As an undergraduate, I was mostly interested in whether a bacterium could store a poem. After spending years studying bacteria, I find myself more interested in the fact that the bacterium has no idea what it is storing.

Biological information and cultural information can look remarkably similar when both are reduced to sequences of letters or bases, but they survive for very different reasons. A bacterial genome is maintained because the organism carrying it needs that information to function. A poem is maintained because someone considers its meaning worth preserving. The two kinds of information can occupy the same molecule without having the same relationship to it.

### Can literature survive without a reader?

This brings the project back to the question Bök was interested in from the beginning. He has described the ambition of *The Xenotext* in terms of creating a work that could survive its author, its civilisation and potentially humanity itself. That is a much bigger claim than simply making DNA store a sequence reliably.

Imagine that the experiment works exactly as intended. Imagine that *D. radiodurans* carries the sequence for thousands or millions of years, passing it between generations as its cells divide. Eventually, perhaps, humans disappear. The bacteria remain, along with the DNA.

Would the poem still exist?

There would certainly still be a sequence. There might still be a protein produced from it, assuming the sequence had not accumulated mutations that changed its function. But there would no longer be anyone who knew that the sequence was supposed to be a poem, let alone someone who could read the English encoded within it.

That raises a question about what we actually mean when we say that literature survives. We normally imagine a poem surviving because its words continue to be read. Shakespeare survives because someone can still open a book and understand what he wrote. The physical paper is incidental. The important thing is that the meaning can be recovered.

Bök's project removes that assumption. He is trying to preserve a poem in a form that could theoretically outlast the people capable of understanding it. The bacterium could carry the sequence perfectly while having absolutely no idea that it is carrying literature.

I didn't think about that when I first wrote about *D. radiodurans* as an undergraduate. At the time, the impressive part seemed to be that a poem could be put into DNA. Now I think the more interesting part is what happens after that. If there is nobody left who can recognise the sequence as a poem, has the poem survived, or has only the information from which we once reconstructed it survived?

Maybe Bök has found a way for literature to outlive its readers. Or maybe he has accidentally demonstrated that readers were always part of what made the literature worth preserving in the first place.
