## The Person I Am in Airports

I’m writing this from Gatwick Airport, waiting to board a flight to Jakarta.

There is something strange about airports when you travel between two lives. They are supposed to be places you pass through, but I have spent enough time in them to feel like they are their own kind of home. You arrive as one version of yourself and, somewhere between security and the departure gate, begin becoming another. At Gatwick, I am still very much the person I have become in England, but I am already thinking about who I will be when I arrive. For a few hours, both versions of my life exist at the same time.

I notice it most when I’m flying to Indonesia. In England, I am used to being the person who does everything herself. I make my own plans, cook for myself, decide when I wake up, where I go and what I spend money on. I can decide to fly somewhere for the weekend because I feel like it, and nobody really needs to know where I am unless I tell them. There is a particular kind of freedom in having a life that is entirely your own, and after years of living this way, I have become very comfortable with it.

Then I get on a plane to Jakarta and, somewhere over the Indian Ocean, that version of me starts to loosen. I know what it will feel like when I arrive. My mum will be waiting for me at the airport with a bag full of my favourite snacks, and by the time I get home I will probably already have been asked whether I am hungry, regardless of how recently I ate. Someone will notice when I go to bed. For a little while, I become somebody’s daughter again rather than just myself. There is something comforting about how easily it happens, but also something strange about realising that a version of me can still appear so quickly after being away for so long.

For a few weeks every year, I become the person I was for the first 17 years of my life. I can spend 11 months in England getting used to making my own decisions, then return to Indonesia and find myself slipping back into routines that I had almost forgotten were routines. I don't consciously decide to become that person again. Being around the people who have known me since I was a child seems to bring her back on its own.

But I know I have changed. There is a whole part of my life that exists in England now, one that my family only knows through the stories I tell them. I have spent years building a life they only see in pieces, so coming back can sometimes feel like bringing two timelines together. They remember the person who left at seventeen, while I have spent the years since becoming someone slightly different. Yet being there makes the distance between those two versions feel much smaller. I can still fall into the same habits and feel completely at ease in a life I no longer live every day.

This is probably why I have become less certain about what I mean when I say I miss home. I know what I miss, but I don't always know where to place it. Sometimes I miss my family, sometimes I miss the familiarity of being somewhere that has known me for most of my life, and sometimes I miss a feeling that is difficult to separate from either place. England is where I have built my adult life, but Indonesia holds the first seventeen years of it. When I am in one, I can find myself thinking about the other.

In England, I have learned to be independent in a way that sometimes feels almost unnatural when I return home. I am used to making decisions without consulting anyone and thinking of my life as something that belongs primarily to me. In Indonesia, I am reminded that this was never really the point of family. You grow up, but you do not necessarily become separate from everyone else. Your life remains partly shared with the people around you, and theirs remains partly shared with you.

At home, people want to know where you are going, when you will be back, whether you have eaten and why you were awake until three in the morning. I used to find those things irritating. Now, after years of living somewhere where nobody needs to know what I am doing unless I tell them, I sometimes see them differently. Some of the things I once thought were intrusive were really just other people paying attention to my life. I still value the freedom I have built in England, but I have also realised that being independent does not mean I have to stop wanting to be looked after.

The strange thing about leaving is that you become very good at building a life somewhere else. You make friends, develop routines and become accustomed to the fact that the people you love are not going to know every ordinary thing that happens to you. Meanwhile, their lives continue without you. My parents get older while I am away. My niece gets taller. There are family stories I wasn't there for and ordinary evenings I never knew happened. Nobody is doing anything wrong. This is simply what happens when the people you love live somewhere you do not.

And then I return to the airport, ready to do it all again. For a few hours, I am neither fully one version of myself nor the other. I am the person who has spent the last several years building a life in England, sitting at a departure gate with a boarding pass to Jakarta, already anticipating the person I will become when I arrive.

There are thousands of people sitting around me at Gatwick right now, all going somewhere. Someone is returning to their family. Someone is leaving a life behind. Someone is moving somewhere new. Everyone is carrying a different history through the same departure gate, even though from the outside we all look like people waiting for a flight.

The flight will take around 18 hours. Somewhere during those hours, I will cross a line on a map, but I don't think that is really what I'm travelling between. I am travelling between two versions of myself: one that I have spent years becoming, and another that I never really stopped being.

For now, both versions of me are waiting at the gate.
