## The Person I Am in Airports

I’m writing this from Gatwick Airport, waiting to board a flight to Jakarta.

There is something strange about airports when you travel between two lives. They are supposed to be places you pass through, but I have spent enough time in them to feel like they are their own kind of home. You arrive as one version of yourself and, somewhere between security and the departure gate, begin becoming another.

I notice it most when I’m flying to Indonesia. In England, I am used to being the person who does everything herself. I make my own plans, cook for myself, decide when I wake up, where I go and what I spend money on. I can hop on a flight to a random European city because I feel like it and nobody really needs to know where I am unless I tell them. There is a particular kind of freedom in that, and over the years I have become very good at being independent.

Then I get on a plane to Jakarta and, somewhere over the Indian Ocean, that version of me starts to loosen. I know what it will feel like when I arrive. My mum will be waiting for me at the airport with a bag full of my favourite snacks. There will be food at home that tastes exactly the way I remember it. I will be asked whether I am hungry even if I have just eaten, and someone will notice what time I went to bed or whether I have eaten breakfast. For a little while, I become somebody’s daughter again rather than just myself. It is comforting, but it also reminds me that there is a whole version of me that only really exists around the people who have known me longest.

Neither version feels fake. They are both me, but they have been shaped by very different lives. In England, I have learned to be independent in a way that sometimes feels almost unnatural when I return home. In Indonesia, I am reminded that independence was never really the point of family. You are not expected to exist entirely on your own. Your life belongs, at least partly, to other people. Sometimes those two ways of living fit together easily. Sometimes they pull in opposite directions. I can miss the freedom of England while also missing the fact that, at home, somebody notices when I haven't eaten.

I think airports make that contradiction more obvious because they are built around transitions. Everyone there is between somewhere and somewhere else. Nobody expects you to explain who you are to the person sitting next to you, or why you are leaving one place to go to another. You simply board the plane and arrive somewhere different. But when you live between places, the transition doesn't really end when the plane lands. You carry parts of each place with you.

I have spent years building a life in England, but I still measure certain things against Indonesia: food, family, language, the way people ask questions, the way care can sometimes feel intrusive until you realise that it is care. There are parts of me that make complete sense in one place and need translating in the other. Sometimes I wonder which version of me is the more authentic one, but I'm not sure there is much point in asking. Maybe neither is more real. Maybe I have simply accumulated different ways of being, each one shaped by where I happened to be and the people around me.

The strange thing about leaving home is that you become very good at arriving somewhere else. You learn how to make friends from scratch, navigate unfamiliar cities, celebrate birthdays through a screen and build routines that nobody from home knows about. You learn that you can miss people and still have a good day. You learn that homesickness isn't always sadness; sometimes it is just the sudden awareness that there is somewhere you belong that you cannot casually get to.

And then you go back, and for a few weeks you become aware of all the things you had forgotten you were missing. I remember what it is like to sit at a table where everyone already knows the story. I remember how easy it is to be understood without explaining yourself. I remember the small frustrations too, because home is not some perfect place preserved in memory. It is just familiar. The same food, the same house, the same people, except that everyone has quietly changed while I have been away.

That is probably the part of leaving that I find hardest. Distance itself is manageable. There are phone calls, video calls and photographs. What is harder to accept is that life keeps happening without you. My parents get older. My niece gets taller. There are family stories I wasn't there for and ordinary evenings I will never know happened. Nobody is doing anything wrong. They are simply living their lives while I am living mine somewhere else.

Then I leave again.

Every goodbye makes the distance feel a little more real. The people at home continue living while I am away, and I continue living while they are there. Neither life stops waiting for the other. Sometimes I wonder whether that is what airports represent to me more than anything else: not really the space between two countries, but the space between the different lives I could be living.

There are thousands of people sitting around me at Gatwick right now, all going somewhere. Someone is returning home. Someone is leaving it. Someone is visiting a parent they haven't seen in years. Someone is moving to a new country. Someone is going back to a place they no longer quite recognise. None of us look particularly different from one another sitting at the gate, but we're all carrying different versions of home with us.

And I'm sitting here with a boarding pass to Jakarta, somewhere between the person I have become and the person I become when I get there.

The flight will take around 18 hours. Somewhere during those hours, I'll cross a line on a map, but I don't think that's really what I'm travelling between.

I'm travelling between versions of myself.

For now, I'm waiting at the gate.
