## The Person I Am in Airports

I’m writing this from Gatwick Airport, waiting to board a flight to Jakarta.

There is something strange about airports when you travel between two lives. They are supposed to be places you pass through, but I have spent enough time in them to feel like they are their own kind of home. You arrive as one version of yourself and, somewhere between security and the departure gate, begin becoming another. At Gatwick, I am still very much the person I have become in England, but I am already thinking about who I will be when I arrive. For a few hours, both versions of my life exist at the same time.

I notice it most when I’m flying to Indonesia. In England, I am used to being the person who does everything herself. I make my own plans, cook for myself, decide when I wake up, where I go, and what I spend money on. I can decide to fly somewhere for the weekend because I feel like it, and nobody really needs to know where I am unless I tell them. There is a particular kind of freedom in having a life that is entirely your own, and after years of living this way, I have become very comfortable with it. Then I get on a plane to Jakarta and, somewhere over the Indian Ocean, that version of me starts to loosen. 

I know what it will feel like when I arrive. My mum will be waiting for me at the airport with a bag full of my favourite snacks, and by the time I get home I will probably already have been asked whether I am hungry, regardless of how recently I ate. For a little while, I become somebody’s daughter again rather than just myself. There is something comforting about how easily it happens, but also something strange about realising that a version of me can still appear so quickly after being away for so long.

For a few weeks every year, I become the person I was for the first 17 years of my life. I can spend 11 months in England getting used to making my own decisions, then return to Indonesia and find myself slipping back into routines that I had almost forgotten were routines. I don't consciously decide to become that person again. Being around the people who have known me since I was a child seems to bring her back on its own. But I know I have changed. There is a whole part of my life that exists in England now, one that my family only knows through the stories I tell them. I have spent years building a life they only see in pieces, so coming back can sometimes feel like bringing two timelines together. They remember the person who left at 17, while I have spent the years since becoming someone slightly different. Yet being there makes the distance between those two versions feel much smaller. I can still fall into the same habits and feel completely at ease in a life I no longer live every day.

This is probably why I have become less certain about what I mean when I say I miss home. I know what I miss, but I don't always know where to place it. Sometimes I miss my family, sometimes I miss the familiarity of being somewhere that has known me for most of my life, and sometimes I miss a feeling that is difficult to separate from either place. England is where I have built my adult life, but Indonesia holds the first seventeen years of it. When I am in one, I can find myself thinking about the other.

In England, I have learned to be independent in a way that sometimes feels almost unnatural when I return home. I am used to making decisions without consulting anyone and thinking of my life as something that belongs primarily to me. In Indonesia, I am reminded that this was never really the point of family. You grow up, but you do not necessarily become separate from everyone else. Your life remains partly shared with the people around you, and theirs remains partly shared with you.

The strange thing about leaving is that you become very good at building a life somewhere else. You make friends, develop routines, and become accustomed to the fact that the people you love are not going to know every ordinary thing that happens to you. Meanwhile, their lives continue without you. My parents get older while I am away. My baby cousin gets taller. There are family stories I wasn't there for and ordinary evenings I never knew happened. This is simply what happens when the people you love live somewhere you do not.

Maybe this is what happens when you spend enough of your life between places. You stop being able to divide yourself neatly between where you came from and where you are now. The person I was at seventeen did not disappear when I left, but she did not stay unchanged either. The person I have become in England is not separate from her; she grew out of her. I carry both with me, even when I am only physically in one place.

That is why airports feel so personal to me. They are one of the few places where I can feel the two parts of my life at once. I am sitting at Gatwick with the life I have built behind me, while already anticipating the person I will become when I arrive in Jakarta. For a few hours, neither has quite taken over.

The flight will take around 18 hours. Somewhere during those hours, I will cross a line on a map, but I don't think that is really what I'm travelling between. I am moving between different parts of my own history, between the person I have become and the person I never completely stopped being. Perhaps that is why I can never quite answer where home is when I am asked. I know the places that have made me who I am. I just don't know if any one of them is enough to contain all of me.

For now, I'm waiting at the gate.
