# On Volunteering

I grew up around the idea that if someone needed help, you helped. In Indonesia, gotong royong was part of the culture around me, and my family volunteered regularly. We donated clothes to orphanages, spent time with the girls there, and I specifically remember sitting with them, playing games and showing them how to braid hair. At school, I volunteered to teach English to Afghan refugees, so by the time I was 16, volunteering felt so ordinary to me that organising something of my own seemed like a natural thing to do.

At school, we learned about the unequal access to healthcare and education across Indonesia, and I became particularly interested in Manggarai, in East Nusa Tenggara. Three friends and I decided to organise a medical mission there, bringing together volunteer doctors and dentists, finding pharmaceutical sponsors, organising medical equipment and travelling to the region ourselves. Before we went, we visited the villages with the doctors and dentists and spoke to village elders about what people needed, and over the course of the trip, we provided free healthcare to around 3,500 people across four villages.

I’m still proud of what we achieved. We persuaded people to donate over £12,000, found medical professionals willing to give their time, and secured medicines and equipment from pharmaceutical companies, while thousands of people received treatment that they might otherwise have struggled to access. At 16, being able to organise something on that scale felt extraordinary, and I had a very straightforward way of understanding what we had done: there was a need, we had found a way to meet it, and people had benefited.

Seven years later, I am about to return to Manggarai, this time carrying clothes and food that I have shipped from Jakarta for people affected by the 2026 earthquake. Coming back to the same region has made me think differently about that first trip, because I have become much more conscious of the assumptions involved in deciding to help. When I decide that I am going to help someone, I am also making a judgement about what they need, what would be useful and whether I am in a position to provide it. I used to barely notice that second decision.

I can see it in some of my earliest volunteering. I remember visiting orphanages and seeing how excited the girls were when we arrived, and at the time that felt like evidence that the visits were worthwhile, but I never thought about what it meant for children in residential care to repeatedly form relationships with volunteers who then disappeared from their lives. UNICEF now advises against volunteering in residential care because of these risks <sup>1</sup>, and although I had no idea about that when I was sitting there braiding hair, I also never asked what happened to those relationships after we went home.

My English teaching was built on an even simpler assumption: I spoke English, so I thought I could teach it, despite being a 14-year-old student with no training in education who doesn’t remember asking what the students actually needed. I had something that seemed useful and treated that as a reason to use it, and looking back, I can see how easily the volunteer becomes the starting point of the intervention: what do I have, what can I do, where can I go? Those questions are natural when you are the person trying to help, but they begin with the volunteer rather than the person receiving the help.

Manggarai was more complicated because there really was a shortage of healthcare, and we did make an effort to understand what the communities needed. We spoke to village elders beforehand, the doctors and dentists were qualified, and the treatment was given, so I don’t think the fact that the mission was temporary somehow cancels the fact that 3,500 people received healthcare. I don’t know that we caused harm, and I’m almost certain we did at least some good.

What I hadn’t really considered was whether the particular way we chose to help was the best use of what we had available. A patient treated during a short visit might need follow-up after the team leaves, a medicine provided during the mission might not be available locally afterwards, and short-term medical missions have raised concerns about continuity of care and whether outside interventions fit into existing local health systems. I don’t know whether any of those problems resulted from our mission specifically, and I don’t want to invent a negative outcome simply because I now know the risks exist; I only know that I wasn’t asking about them at the time.

There is another question I still haven’t done the maths on: what did it actually cost to fly volunteer doctors and dentists into Manggarai, once you count the flights, logistics and the time people spent fundraising for a particular trip, and what would that same money have funded if it had gone directly to local clinics or health workers already living in those communities? I don’t have the comparison, even now, but I’m fairly sure I would find the answer uncomfortable because visiting doctors are, almost by definition, a more expensive way to deliver care than people already working there. I never asked whether the extra cost was what made the difference, or whether it simply made the difference feel more tangible to us because we could see ourselves in it.

That question has changed how I think about volunteering. A successful outcome tells me that something happened, but it doesn’t necessarily tell me whether it was the most useful thing that could have happened with the resources available. We could count the people we treated, the money we raised and the hours that doctors gave us, but we couldn’t see the alternatives: the patients who might have been treated by a local clinic, the infrastructure that might have been supported, or the work that could have continued after we left.

This is also why I find the language of “giving back” slightly uncomfortable now. It can make the act sound simpler than it is, as though having something to give is enough to establish that giving it will be beneficial. Sometimes it probably is. Sometimes the most useful thing an outsider can do is provide money, skills or labour, and sometimes the person who sees the need really is the person best placed to respond to it. But deciding to intervene still means making a judgement about someone else’s circumstances, and I think that judgement deserves more scrutiny than I gave it when I was younger.

There is no way to remove that uncertainty completely. Even when you ask the right questions, you are still making a decision based on incomplete information, and sometimes there simply isn’t time to investigate every possible consequence before acting.

I am thinking about that now because I am about to return to Manggarai. The earthquake has created an immediate need for food, clothing and other basic necessities, and although aid has been reaching affected communities, distribution has been gradual in some areas because of the geography and limited transport routes. The Indonesian government has also said that foreign assistance was not needed at that stage, choosing to rely on national capacity and resources. Watching the situation unfold, I found it difficult to simply wait when I could get things to Manggarai and knew there were people who had lost homes and needed basic necessities.

At the same time, I know enough now to question whether sending clothes and food is the best thing I could do. I don’t know whether the communities I reach will need exactly what I have brought, whether other supplies are already arriving, or whether the money could have been used more effectively somewhere else. I can ask some of those questions, but I can’t answer all of them from Jakarta, and there is a point at which trying to eliminate the possibility of getting it wrong becomes another way of avoiding the decision altogether.

That is the part I find hardest. I have learned to be more suspicious of my own certainty about what other people need, but I still have to make judgements about what I think will help. In an emergency, not acting is also a choice, and sometimes the information available to you is simply not good enough to make that choice with confidence.

I’m going back to Manggarai with a much less certain idea of what it means to help than I had the first time. Seven years ago, I arrived knowing what I could offer and assumed that was enough; this time I am carrying things I hope will be useful while knowing that I may have misunderstood what is needed. I may find that what I brought was genuinely useful, or I may discover that there was a better way to have helped, and I think I have to be willing to learn from either outcome.

I’m still proud of what we did when I was 16, and I don’t need to decide that it was either entirely right or secretly harmful in order to question it. What has changed is that I no longer see the willingness to help, or even the ability to help, as proof that I understand what someone else needs. Sometimes helping means knowing what to give, but before that, it means accepting that you might be wrong about what giving should look like.
