# On Volunteering

I grew up around the idea that if someone needed help, you helped. In Indonesia, gotong royong was part of the culture around me, and my family volunteered regularly. We donated clothes to orphanages, spent time with the girls there, and I specifically remember sitting with them, playing games and showing them how to braid hair. At school, I volunteered to teach English to Afghan refugees. By the time I was 17, volunteering felt so ordinary to me that organising something of my own seemed like a natural thing to do.

Seven years later, I am about to return to Manggarai, in East Nusa Tenggara, for a very different reason. In 2019, when I was 17, three friends and I organised a medical mission there after learning about unequal access to healthcare and education across Indonesia. This time, I am travelling through the region carrying clothes and food that I have shipped from Jakarta for people affected by the 2026 earthquake. I have been thinking a lot about that first trip while deciding what to do now, because I no longer find the question of whether I want to help particularly difficult. I find the question of whether I know what help should look like much harder.

At 17, the medical mission felt like a straightforward response to a straightforward need. Three friends and I brought together volunteer doctors and dentists, found pharmaceutical sponsors, organised medical equipment and travelled to Manggarai ourselves. Before we went, we visited the villages with the doctors and dentists and spoke to village elders about what people needed. Over the course of the trip, we provided free healthcare to around 3,500 people across four villages.

I'm still proud of what we achieved. We persuaded people to donate over £12,000, found medical professionals willing to give their time, and secured medicines and equipment from pharmaceutical companies. Thousands of people received treatment that they might otherwise have struggled to access. I don't know that we caused harm, and I'm almost certain we did at least some good. What has changed is the question I ask afterwards: was doing some good enough for me to assume that we had done the right thing?

I used to think that seeing a need and having the ability to respond to it was enough. If someone needed something I could provide, why wouldn't I provide it? I didn't spend much time considering what made me qualified to decide that my particular contribution was useful, or what happened once the intervention was over. I was much more interested in whether I could do something than in whether I was the person who should be doing it.

I can see that in some of my earliest volunteering. I remember visiting orphanages and seeing how excited the girls were when we arrived. At the time, that felt like proof that the visits were worthwhile. I never thought about what it meant for children in residential care to repeatedly form relationships with volunteers who then disappeared from their lives. UNICEF now advises against volunteering in residential care because of these risks. I had no idea about that when I was sitting there braiding hair, but I also never asked what happened after we went home.

My English teaching was built on an even simpler assumption: I spoke English, so I thought I could teach it. I was a 14-year-old student with no training in education, and I don't remember asking what the students actually needed. I had something that seemed useful and treated that as a reason to use it. Looking back, I can see how easily the volunteer becomes the starting point of the intervention: what do I have, what can I do, where can I go? Those questions are very different from asking what is needed, who is already providing it and whether my involvement would actually improve the situation.

Manggarai makes this harder to think about because we did make some of those checks. There was a genuine shortage of healthcare. We spoke to village elders beforehand, and the doctors and dentists were qualified. The treatment was given, and 3,500 people were seen. The fact that the mission was temporary does not erase those outcomes.

But I had never really questioned the intervention itself. A patient treated during a short visit might need follow-up after the team leaves. A medicine provided during the mission might not be available locally afterwards. Short-term medical missions have also raised concerns about continuity of care and whether outside interventions fit into existing local health systems. I don't know whether any of those problems resulted from our mission specifically, and I don't want to invent a negative outcome simply because I now know the risks exist. What I do know is that I wasn't asking about them at the time.

There is another question I still haven't done the maths on. What did it actually cost to fly volunteer doctors and dentists into Manggarai, once you count the flights, logistics and the time people spent fundraising for a particular trip? What would that same money have funded if it had gone directly to local clinics or health workers already living in those communities? I don't have the comparison, even now. I'm fairly sure I would find the answer uncomfortable, because visiting doctors are, almost by definition, a more expensive way to deliver care than people already working there. I never asked whether the extra cost was what made the difference, or whether it simply made the difference feel more tangible to us because we could see ourselves in it.

That is the part of volunteering I find harder to ignore now. A visible outcome tells me that something happened, but it doesn't tell me whether it was the best thing that could have happened with the time, money and expertise available. I used to count people reached, money raised and hours given because those were things I could see. I rarely considered the alternatives, or what happened after we left.

I am thinking about all of this now because the earthquake has put me in a situation where I have to make another decision about helping, but with much less time to deliberate. Aid has been reaching affected communities, although distribution has been gradual in some areas because of the geography and limited transport routes. The Indonesian government has also said that foreign assistance was not needed at that stage, choosing to rely on national capacity and resources. Watching the situation unfold, I found it difficult to simply wait. I could get things to Manggarai, and there were people who had lost homes and needed basic necessities.

I also know enough now to question whether sending clothes and food is the best thing I could do. I don't know whether the communities I reach will need exactly what I have brought, whether other supplies are already arriving, or whether the money could have been used more effectively somewhere else. There is a limit to how much of that uncertainty I can resolve from Jakarta, though. If I spend weeks trying to work out the perfect intervention, the people who need food today don't benefit from my deliberation.

That leaves me somewhere less comfortable than where I started. I still think carefully about who has identified a need, who is already responding and whether I am actually useful, but those questions don't always produce a clear answer. Sometimes there is enough information to know what to do. Sometimes there isn't, and refusing to act is a decision too.

I'm going back to Manggarai with much more uncertainty than I had the first time. Seven years ago, I arrived convinced that the ability to help was itself a good reason to act. This time I'm carrying things I hope will be useful while knowing that hope is not evidence that I have chosen correctly. I may find that I got some of it wrong, or that what I brought was genuinely useful, and I will have to be honest about whichever of those things I find.
