## When Helping Isn’t Helpful

I grew up thinking volunteering was simply something you did. In Indonesia, gotong royong was part of the culture around me: people helped their neighbours, contributed what they could and showed up when something needed doing. My family volunteered regularly, so I never really thought of it as volunteering. It was just what you did when someone else needed help.

For as long as I can remember, my family would donate clothes to orphanages. We would spend time with the girls there too, and I remember sitting with them, playing games and showing them how to braid hair. At school, I volunteered to teach English to Afghan refugees. When I was older, three friends and I organised something much bigger. We brought together volunteer doctors and dentists, found pharmaceutical sponsors, organised medical equipment and travelled to Manggarai in East Nusa Tenggara. Before we went, we visited the villages with the doctors and dentists and spoke to village elders about what people needed. Over the course of the trip, we provided free healthcare to around 3,500 people across four villages.

I’m proud of what we achieved. At seventeen, it felt extraordinary to have helped organise something on that scale. We had persuaded people to donate medicines, found medical professionals willing to give their time and managed to get the equipment and volunteers to communities with limited access to healthcare. Thousands of people received treatment that they might otherwise have struggled to access. But as I’ve grown older, I’ve also learned more about how easily well-intentioned aid can go wrong. That has made me look back at the trip with more questions than I had at seventeen. I don’t know that we caused harm, and in many cases we almost certainly did some good. What I question now is whether doing some good was enough for me to assume that we had done the right thing.

I used to think that seeing a need and having the ability to respond to it was enough. If someone needed something I could provide, why wouldn’t I provide it? I didn’t spend much time considering what made me qualified to decide that my particular contribution was useful, or what happened once the intervention was over. Those questions seem obvious to me now, partly because I have since learned how easily well-intentioned volunteering can create problems that are invisible to the person doing it.

I think about the orphanage visits differently now. I remember the girls being excited when we arrived, and I interpreted that as evidence that our visits were worthwhile. I didn’t think about what it meant for a child in residential care to repeatedly meet adults who took an interest in them and then disappeared from their lives. UNICEF advises against volunteering in residential care because children can form attachments to volunteers who subsequently leave. I had no idea about that when I was sitting there braiding hair, but I also never asked what happened to those relationships when we went home.

My English teaching was built on an even simpler assumption. I spoke English, so I thought I could teach it. I was a school student with no training in education, and I don’t remember asking whether the students actually needed what I was offering. I had something that seemed useful and treated that as a reason to use it. Looking back, I can see how easily the volunteer can become the starting point of the intervention: what do I have, what can I do, where can I go? The more important question should be what is needed, whether I am the right person to provide it and whether someone else is already better placed to do so.

Manggarai was different because there was a genuine shortage of healthcare and we did make an effort to understand what the communities needed. The doctors and dentists were qualified, we spoke to village elders before the trip and the treatment itself was real. Reaching 3,500 people was a substantial outcome. I don’t think the fact that the mission was temporary somehow cancels that.

But temporary help raises questions I hadn’t thought much about at the time. A patient treated during a short visit might need follow-up after the team leaves. A medicine provided during the mission might not be available locally afterwards. Research into short-term medical missions has raised real concerns about continuity of care and whether outside interventions actually align with local priorities and health systems. I don’t know whether any of that happened because of our mission specifically. I wasn’t there long enough to know, and I don’t want to invent a negative outcome simply because I now know the risks exist. What I do know is that I wasn’t asking about any of it at the time. I was thinking about whether we could get the doctors there, how many medicines we had and how many people we could reach. A larger number felt like a better outcome, and at seventeen I had little reason to question that.

There’s a version of this question I still haven’t actually done the arithmetic on: what flying volunteer doctors and dentists into Manggarai actually cost, once you count the flights, the logistics and the time donors spent fundraising for a specific trip rather than a specific outcome, against what that same money would have funded if it had gone directly to local clinics or health workers already living in those communities. I don’t have that comparison, even now. I’m fairly confident I would come out of it a little uncomfortable, because visiting doctors are, almost by definition, a more expensive way to deliver care than the people already there. I never asked whether the extra cost of flying qualified strangers somewhere was what actually made the difference, or whether it just made the difference feel more like ours.

There was also something satisfying about being able to see the result. We organised the trip, people donated money and equipment, doctors treated patients and then we could go home knowing that thousands of people had been seen. That made it easy to feel that we had done something worthwhile. I don’t think there is anything wrong with feeling good about helping, but I now realise that the feeling itself tells me very little about whether the help was actually useful.

That has changed how I think about volunteering. Before offering something now, I want to know who has identified the need, who is already responding to it and what role an outsider can realistically play. Sometimes that will mean volunteering directly. Sometimes it will mean giving money to an organisation with people on the ground who understand the situation better than I do. It might mean doing something less satisfying because the useful contribution is behind the scenes rather than something I can personally see.

And sometimes there isn’t time to work all of this out.

I am thinking about that now because I am about to return to Manggarai. Seven years after that first medical trip, I am travelling through the same region again, this time carrying clothes and food that I have shipped from Jakarta for people affected by the 2026 earthquake.

The circumstances are very different. This is an immediate disaster, and people have lost homes and access to basic necessities. Aid has been reaching affected communities, but distribution has been gradual in some areas because of the geography and limited transport routes. The Indonesian government has also said that foreign assistance was not needed at that stage, choosing to rely on national capacity and resources. Watching the situation unfold, I found it difficult to simply wait. I could get things to Manggarai, and there were people who needed things now.

If I spend weeks trying to determine the perfect way to help, the people who need food or clothes today don’t benefit from my careful deliberation. But acting quickly also means accepting that I may be wrong. I don’t necessarily know which supplies are most needed in the communities I will reach, whether what I have sent duplicates other donations or whether the money could have been used more effectively elsewhere. In an emergency, there may be no option that gives you complete information before you have to make a decision.

That is probably the part I find hardest. I have spent years learning not to let my own ability to help be reason enough on its own, only to find myself in a situation where not acting also feels like a decision. I don’t think the answer is to abandon that caution. I also don’t think caution should become an excuse for doing nothing while people are waiting for basic necessities.

So I am going back to Manggarai with a slightly uncomfortable combination of confidence and doubt. I am confident that there are people who need help. I am less confident that I always know what that help should look like. Seven years ago, I thought uncertainty was something to overcome before acting. Now I think it is something I have to carry with me while I decide.

I am still proud of what we did when I was seventeen. I am also glad that I can look back at it and ask questions that I wasn’t asking then. The lesson I take from those experiences isn’t that volunteering is dangerous or that good intentions are meaningless. It is that wanting to help gives me no special authority to decide what help should look like.

I used to start with what I could give. Now I try to start with what is needed, even when the answer is inconvenient or unclear. In Manggarai this week, I probably won’t know whether I have got everything right. I will still have to decide what to do, and this time I will be paying much more attention to the assumptions behind that decision.

I don’t have a tidy example yet of that actually working, because I’m writing this before the trip, not after it. Maybe that’s the more honest way to end it. If the argument is that uncertainty is something to carry rather than resolve before acting, an essay that closes on a confirmed good outcome would be exactly the kind of neat resolution I’m arguing against.
