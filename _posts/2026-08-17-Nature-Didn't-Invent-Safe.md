## Nature Didn't Invent Safe

For years, manuka honey has been used in hospitals for wound care and sold as a natural alternative to conventional antiseptics. Its antimicrobial activity is well established, but the mechanism is more complicated you'd might think. Methylglyoxal has long been considered a major contributor, yet earlier studies found that manuka honey could retain antimicrobial activity even after methylglyoxal was neutralised. Recent work from Aston University has continued to investigate the other components involved, suggesting that its activity comes from several factors rather than a single molecule.¹

Manuka honey is an interesting example of how we decide what to trust. A new synthetic drug has to establish its safety through clinical evidence and *years* of use, while natural substances can arrive with some of that trust already attached to them. Calling something “natural” tells us where it came from, but says little about whether it is safe or effective. Part of that may simply be familiarity: if something has been used for generations, it can seem safer because we have had more time to observe it. That history can provide useful evidence, but only about that particular substance and how it has been used. Being natural, by itself, tells us very little about whether something is safe or effective.

I spend my working days trying to understand how a lab-designed antimicrobial compound kills MRSA. It has to be assessed by the same basic questions as any other antimicrobial: does it work, if so, how, and what else does it affect? The fact that one came from a lab and the other came from a bee doesn’t answer any of them.

### What honey is doing
Honey has several antimicrobial mechanisms acting simultaneously. Its high sugar concentration draws water out of bacterial cells by osmosis, while its acidity creates conditions in which many bacteria struggle to grow. When honey is diluted, the enzyme glucose oxidase can generate hydrogen peroxide, which contributes to its antimicrobial activity. Manuka honey contains high levels of dihydroxyacetone, which gradually converts into methylglyoxal as the honey matures. Methylglyoxal is highly reactive and can damage bacterial proteins and other cellular components.¹

Honey is a complex mixture whose composition varies between samples, so its antimicrobial activity cannot be reduced to a single compound or mechanism. Methylglyoxal is clearly important, but it does not explain everything. Researchers can isolate individual compounds and test what they do on their own, but that does not necessarily tell us how they behave when they are all present together. The activity of the whole mixture may be different from the sum of its individual parts.

This is quite different from how we understand many conventional antibiotics. Penicillin, for example, targets proteins involved in building the bacterial cell wall, a structure that human cells do not have. This gives the drug a degree of selectivity: it can disrupt an essential bacterial structure without targeting an equivalent structure in human cells.² Honey has no equivalent single target. Its antimicrobial activity appears to come from several physical and chemical properties acting together. It is strange, then, that the labels often suggest the opposite: the engineered molecule is “chemical” and suspicious, while a mixture of reactive compounds, acid and concentrated sugar is “natural” and “soothing.”

More importantly, antimicrobial activity is not the same thing as clinical effectiveness. Showing that a substance inhibits bacteria in the lab is only one part of establishing that it is a useful treatment. A compound can kill bacteria in a dish but fail in a patient because it cannot reach the infection at a sufficient concentration, cannot penetrate the relevant tissue, is unstable in the body or causes unacceptable toxicity. Even when a treatment does improve outcomes, we still need to know which patients benefit, at what dose, and under what conditions.


### The rest of what nature makes
Nature has given us some of our best medicines and some of our worst poisons, which makes it a poor category for judging safety. *Streptomyces* bacteria produce many clinically important antibiotics, as well as geosmin, the compound responsible for the earthy smell after rain.³ On the other hand, *Clostridium botulinum* produces one the most potent toxins known, botulinum neurotoxins, and death cap mushrooms contain amatoxins that can cause severe liver damage and death. Nature does not distinguish between what is useful to us and what isn’t.

The instinct to associate natural things with safety isn’t completely irrational. Humans have accumulated a great deal of knowledge about plants, fungi and other natural substances through observation and experience. I grew up around Indonesian herbal remedies, or *jamu*, which have been used for generations, with knowledge about particular plants and preparations passed down over time. If a substance has been used in a particular way for generations without an obvious pattern of harm, that tells us something. But it tells us about that substance and that use, not about everything else that came from nature. Our familiarity with nature is also selective: we tend to encounter the plants, foods, and medicines that have proved useful, while most of the dangerous ones remain outside everyday life.

Chemistry makes the distinction less useful still. Vitamin C made in a lab is chemically identical to vitamin C in a lemon, and aspirin produced by a pharmaceutical company is the same molecule regardless of its history in willow bark. Once you have isolated and characterised a molecule, whether it came from a plant or a lab doesn’t change what that molecule is. That doesn’t mean natural and synthetic substances are always interchangeable. A crude natural mixture can contain many compounds in varying concentrations, while a purified drug can be manufactured to a defined composition and dose. Those differences can affect efficacy and safety.

The synthetic side isn’t automatically safer either. Thalidomide was marketed in the 1950s as a treatment for morning sickness and caused severe birth defects in thousands of children before the scale of the harm became clear.⁴ A molecule coming out of a laboratory is no guarantee that we understand what it will do in a human body. And the boundary between natural and synthetic is often less clear than it sounds: a molecule may be discovered in a microorganism, isolated and characterised, chemically modified to improve its properties and eventually produced synthetically. By the time it becomes a drug, asking whether it is “natural” doesn’t tell us much about it. Evidence gives us a much better basis for judging something than familiarity ever can.

### What earns trust
We tend to use familiarity as a shortcut for safety. Something that has been around for hundreds of years feels less threatening than something invented last Tuesday, but familiarity tells us little about how well a substance has actually been studied. Manuka honey is a useful example because both things can be true at once: it has a long history of use in wound care and well-established antimicrobial properties, while researchers are still working out which compounds contribute to those effects and how they interact. The Aston study doesn’t suddenly make honey unsafe; it adds another piece to an explanation that was already incomplete.

I work with synthetic antimicrobials because someone designed a molecule to do a particular job against a bacterium. Manuka honey has antimicrobial properties for entirely different reasons, without anyone designing it for that purpose. But when I think about the two side by side, their origins seem like the least useful thing to compare. What matters is what they do, how they do it, and what evidence we have for using them safely.

Perhaps that is the more useful way to think about “natural” medicine too: not as something that deserves more trust, but as something that deserves to be understood.
