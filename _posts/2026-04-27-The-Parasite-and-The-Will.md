# The Parasite and The Will

Discussions of free will usually centre on whether we can act otherwise in a physically determined world. It starts from the assumption that our desires are ours, taking for granted that internal desires belong to the individual experiencing them. A parasite that makes a rat want to approach a cat challenges that premise, by demonstrating that the desire itself can be written by someone else. If that is possible, the question of whether we are free to act on our desires comes after a more fundamental question: whether our desires belong to us at all

In 2000, researchers at the University of Oxford offered rats nesting sites across an enclosure scented with various animal odours. Uninfected rats avoided the cat scent entirely. Rats carrying the single-celled parasite *Toxoplasma gondii* lost that fear and were drawn towards the cat-scented area, a result the authors titled "fatal attraction".<sup>1</sup> Because *Toxoplasma* reproduces sexually only within cat intestines, suppressing a rat's instinct to flee increases the chance of predation, allowing the parasite to complete its life cycle. Approximately one third of the human population carries dormant *Toxoplasma* infections without symptoms.<sup>2</sup> Whether the parasite changes human behaviour in the same way remains an open question in clinical research.

Later work in rodents indicates that this loss of fear was specific: the animals stopped fearing cat odour while continuing to avoid other predators, a difference traced to the amygdala, a brain region that processes fear.<sup>3</sup> *Toxoplasma* carries genes for an enzyme that helps produce dopamine, a chemical messenger linked to pleasure and pursuit, suggesting a chemical route for altering behaviour, though proof that this enzyme causes the behavioural shift is still lacking.<sup>4</sup> In 2020, research on mice showed that reduced fear applied to several predators and stemmed from general brain inflammation instead of a targeted takeover of specific brain circuits.<sup>5</sup> Studies in humans show mixed findings. Jaroslav Flegr linked dormant infections to slower reaction times and higher rates of traffic accidents,<sup>6</sup> whereas a long-term study following individuals from birth in New Zealand found no link between infection and psychological differences.<sup>7</sup> Behavioural changes clearly occur in rodents, whereas findings in humans show unconfirmed, broad brain changes rather than the insertion of targeted desires.

Commensal microorganisms within the gut exert wide-ranging physical influences that poses similar challenges for researchers. Mice raised without any gut bacteria show abnormal hormone responses to stress and display anxious behaviour, changes linked to altered brain development.<sup>8</sup> Transferring gut bacteria from active or calm donor mice into these sterile animals shifts their behaviour to match the donors.<sup>9</sup> Administering specific strains of *Lactobacillus* reduces anxiety and changes receptors in the brain; cutting the vagus nerve, the main communication line between the gut and brain, blocked this effect.<sup>10</sup> In humans, the gut bacteria of those with depression differ from those of healthy individuals, and faecal microbiota transplantation from depressed patients into rodents produces depressive behaviours.<sup>11</sup> These human observations show association rather than causation, because depression changes diet and medication, factors that independently reshape bacterial populations.

Evolutionary biologists propose that gut bacteria may have evolutionary interests separate from the health of their host. Different species of bacteria thrive on different foods, creating evolutionary incentives to sway host cravings using chemical signals.<sup>12</sup> While biologically plausible, this idea lacks testing in humans. Such effects resemble familiar bodily influences. A mood change caused by gut bacteria resembles the irritability of sleep deprivation or the alertness from morning coffee. A business executive drinking coffee before a meeting experiences an altered mental state without treating that extra focus as an "alien" takeover. Treating bacterial activity like any other physical process blurs the line between bodily influence and manipulation, showing that an outside cause alone cannot separate genuine desire from forced action.

Common intuition suggests the infected rat acts without freedom because the fatal attraction comes from the parasite instead of the host. Defending this difference becomes difficult once we examine what makes an urge our own. All biological desires stem from physical causes, such as genetic inheritance and shifting hormone levels. The infected rat feels no internal struggle, moving towards the predator with the direct feeling of a genuine desire.

Philosophers often separate genuine free will from outside control by looking at our ability to reflect. Harry Frankfurt argued that free will requires layered desires: an agent is free when able to examine basic impulses and decide whether to endorse or reject them.<sup>13</sup> On this view, the rat lacks freedom because it cannot reflect on its urges, whereas a person whose mood shifts through gut bacteria remains free if thoughtful reflection approves the action. This view faces a weakness when an outside influence shapes the reflection itself, causing the person to endorse an urge under the sway of that same force. Derk Pereboom illustrated this difficulty through a series of cases ranging from direct brain control by scientists to ordinary physical causes, concluding that if deliberate manipulation destroys moral responsibility, then standard physical causation must do so as well.<sup>14</sup> Daniel Dennett rejected this conclusion by focusing on reasons-responsiveness. In Dennett's view, freedom worth wanting requires the capacity to recognise good reasons to act differently and adjust behaviour accordingly, making the origin of a desire less important than whether it changes when presented with reasons.<sup>15</sup>

Responsiveness to reasons provides a clear standard for separating helpful influences from those that undermine our agency. Influences that work through conscious reasoning—such as sound arguments or understandable feelings—preserve freedom by leaving our critical judgment intact. Influences that bypass conscious thought by locking desires in place without room for reconsidering weaken our control. *Toxoplasma* sits at this extreme. By shutting down the natural fear responses that keep a rat alive, the parasite silences the inner warnings that would otherwise guide its choices. Finding where human gut bacteria or dormant infections sit on this spectrum remains an empirical question awaiting direct tests.

Recognising hidden bodily influences actually widens our freedom of action. Jon Elster pointed to the story of Ulysses binding himself to the ship's mast, a deliberate strategy allowing the sailor to hear the Sirens while preventing a disastrous response.<sup>16</sup> People use similar tactics when keeping junk food out of the house or maintaining regular sleep hours to protect their mood. Discoveries about gut bacteria add new tools to this familiar practice, treating internal impulses as physical events we can manage. Changing one's diet to foster beneficial bacteria fits this approach, though firm clinical evidence that diet can precisely steer mood remains in its early stages.

The lesson of the infected rat is that our internal desires can stem from causes structured to serve another organism's survival. Freedom in a physical world is a continuous practice rather than a fixed possession. Individuals learn which bodily forces act upon them, then shape their surroundings so their daily choices follow reasons they endorse. The science of parasites and gut bacteria reinforces this need, calling for close attention to the biological roots of our strongest desires.
