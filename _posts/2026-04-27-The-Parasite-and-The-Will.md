# The Parasite and The Will

Discussions of free will usually centre on whether we can act otherwise in a physically determined world. It starts from the assumption that our desires are ours, taking for granted that internal desires belong to the individual experiencing them. A parasite that makes a rat want to approach a cat challenges that premise, by demonstrating that the desire itself can be written by someone else. If that is possible, the question of whether we are free to act on our desires comes after a more fundamental question: whether our desires belong to us at all

In 2000, researchers at the University of Oxford offered rats nesting sites across an enclosure scented with various animal odours. Uninfected rodents avoided the cat scent entirely. Rats carrying the protozoan parasite *Toxoplasma gondii* lost that aversion and were drawn towards the cat-scented area, a result the authors titled "fatal attraction".<sup>1</sup> Because *Toxoplasma* reproduces sexually only within cat intestines, a rat that stops fearing cats is more likely to be eaten by one, and the parasite is more likely to complete its lifecycle. Approximately one third of the human population carries latent *Toxoplasma* infections without overt clinical symptoms.<sup>2</sup> Whether the parasite exerts an equivalent behavioural alteration in humans remains unresolved across clinical trials.

Later work in rodents indicate that this loss of fear was selective: aversion to cat odour vanishes while defensive reactions to other predatory cues persist, a divergence localised to the basolateral amygdala.<sup>3</sup> *Toxoplasma* carries genes encoding a tyrosine hydroxylase homologue involved in dopamine synthesis, providing a plausible mechanism for neurochemical intervention, though proof that this enzyme drives the behavioural shift is still lacking.<sup>4</sup> In 2020, murine studies demonstrated that diminished aversion extended to multiple predators and correlated with generalised neuroinflammation rather than dedicated circuit hijacking.<sup>5</sup> Human investigations yield conflicting findings. Jaroslav Flegr linked latent toxoplasmosis to slower reaction times and elevated traffic accident rates,<sup>6</sup> whereas longitudinal analysis of a New Zealand birth cohort established no reliable association between seropositivity and neuropsychiatric variations.<sup>7</sup> Rodent behavioural modification clearly occurs, while human manifestations lack robust replication, indicating generalised neural alterations rather than the insertion of specific desires.

Commensal microorganisms within the gastrointestinal tract present broader physiological influences accompanied by comparable methodological boundaries. Germ-free mice show abnormal endocrine responses to stress and show anxiety-like behaviour, anomalies rooted in altered neurodevelopment and synaptic plasticity.<sup>8</sup> Colonising these germ-free animals with microbiota sourced from donors possessing distinct behavioural profiles shifts recipient conduct toward the donor phenotype.<sup>9</sup> Administering specific *Lactobacillus* strains dampens anxious phenotypes and alters central GABA receptor expression; severing the subdiaphragmatic vagus nerve removed the effect, confirming a route from gut to brain.<sup>10</sup> In humans, the gut bacteria of those with depression differ from those of others, and faecal microbiota transplantation from these patients into rodents induces depressive phenotypes.<sup>11</sup> Clinical observations remain predominantly correlational, as depressive states inherently alter dietary intake and pharmacological regimens, factors that restructure microbial ecologies independently.

Evolutionary biologists propose that enteric microorganisms harbour selective interests distinct from host fitness. Competing microbial taxa benefit whenever host consumption favours their specific nutritional requirements, creating evolutionary incentives to influence appetite through neuroendocrine signalling and cytokine release.<sup>12</sup> While biologically plausible, this hypothesis awaits rigorous human trials. These interactions parallel ordinary metabolic variables: an affective shift driven by commensal bacteria mirrors the irritability of sleep deprivation or the alertness following morning caffeine. An executive drinking coffee before a negotiation experiences externally modified cognition without regarding that heightened focus as alien authorship. Grouping microbial activity with routine physiological drivers obscures the boundary between bodily influence and outright manipulation, establishing that external causation alone fails to distinguish authentic motivation from coerced desire.

An intuitive philosophical assessment of the infected rodent treats its conduct as unfree on the grounds that the fatal attraction originates with the parasite rather than the host. Defending this distinction becomes conceptually treacherous once the criteria for ownership undergo scrutiny. Biological desires arise from deterministic antecedents including genetic predispositions and fluctuating endocrinological states. The infected rat experiences no coercive friction, navigating toward the predator with the subjective immediacy of genuine appetite.

Philosophical accounts of autonomous agency delineate authentic volition from external compulsion by evaluating reflective structure. Harry Frankfurt argued that freedom of will requires hierarchical volition: an agent possesses freedom when capable of evaluating first-order desires and forming second-order endorsements or repudiations of those urges.<sup>13</sup> Under this formulation, the rodent lacks freedom because it lacks reflective architecture, whereas a human subject experiencing microbial mood alterations retains autonomy if higher-order deliberation ratifies the resulting conduct. This hierarchical model encounters a critical vulnerability when external manipulation reaches the reflective mechanism itself, causing the second-order endorsement to be generated by the very force under evaluation. Derk Pereboom illustrated this tension through his four-case manipulation argument, tracing an unbroken continuum from direct neuroscientific control to deterministic physical causation, concluding that if deliberate manipulation undermines moral accountability, universal physical determinism must do so likewise.<sup>14</sup> Daniel Dennett rejected this equivalence by anchoring autonomy in reasons-responsiveness. Under Dennett's framework, freedom worth wanting requires the capacity to comprehend salient reasons to act differently and revise behaviour accordingly, rendering the causal provenance of an inclination secondary to its rational plasticity.<sup>15</sup>

Reasons-responsiveness provides a principled standard for distinguishing benign influences from subversions of agency. Influences operating through deliberative faculties—such as persuasive arguments or identifiable emotional states—sustain autonomy by leaving critical assessment functional. Influences operating around deliberation by fixing desires impervious to rational revision actively diminish agency. *Toxoplasma* resides at this destructive extreme. By disabling the innate neural fear responses that preserve rodent life, the parasite silences the affective alarms that would otherwise govern behavioural choice. Mapping precisely where human microbiota or latent infections fall along this spectrum constitutes an open empirical programme requiring direct experimental verification.

Recognising covert physiological drivers expands an agent's deliberative control. Jon Elster invoked the Homeric account of Ulysses binding himself to the ship's mast, an intentional precommitment enabling the voyager to hear the Sirens while foreclosing catastrophic action.<sup>16</sup> Contemporary agents employ analogous strategies when clearing domestic spaces of unhealthy food or enforcing strict sleep schedules to stabilise emotional equilibrium. Microbiological discoveries simply supply new parameters for an established cognitive discipline: analysing internal impulses as contingent biological events subject to strategic intervention. Modifying dietary regimens to cultivate specific microbial populations fits naturally within this precommitment paradigm, though clinical proof of targeted mood modification via dietary microbial engineering remains nascent.

The difficulty presented by the infected rodent lies in the realisation that its internal appetites possess causes engineered to serve an external organism's reproductive imperatives. Autonomy within a biologically determined world constitutes an ongoing discipline rather than a static state. Agents systematically investigate the somatic forces operating upon them, subsequently structuring their environments so deliberate choices align with endorsed reasons. Encountering parasitic manipulators and enteric colonies sharpens this imperative, demanding vigilance regarding the biological origins of our most compelling appetites.
