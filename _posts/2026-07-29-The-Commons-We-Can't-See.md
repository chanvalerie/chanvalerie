## The Commons We Can't See


In the summer of 1928, Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming left his laboratory bench for a 14-day holiday. When he returned, Penicillium notatum had grown in an uncovered culture plate, and to his surprise, a clear zone had formed around the mould where the surrounding bacteria had been inhibited <sup>1</sup>. He had accidentally discovered penicillin, a substance that could eventually be developed into a drug and drawn upon whenever bacterial infections threatened human life.

Call it a commons and you might get funny looks from people who take the word seriously. A commons is usually understood as something physical that can be observed and depleted, such as a pasture or fishing ground. Antibiotic effectiveness is different in that you cannot watch it deplete; what is being depleted is a probability rather than a stockpile. Each use can shift the odds in favour of resistant bacteria, gradually reducing the likelihood that the same drug will remain effective when it is needed.

Antibiotic stewardship attempts to manage precisely this problem, yet it remains difficult to achieve consistently. I do not think this is a consequence of insufficient knowledge – most clinicians are well aware of the statistics surrounding antibiotic resistance. The greater challenge is what happens when that knowledge has to compete with the immediate pressures involved in clinical decision-making.

Each course of antibiotics kills susceptible bacteria while allowing resistant bacteria to survive and multiply <sup>2</sup>. A single prescription may have little measurable effect, but billions of prescriptions exert cumulative selective pressure on bacterial populations, increasing the prevalence of resistance over time. The resulting loss of antibiotic effectiveness is difficult to observe as there is no stockpile to measure and no obvious point at which it runs out. It also does not occur at the same rate everywhere: a drug can remain highly effective in one hospital while resistance has made it considerably less useful elsewhere. Resistance develops unevenly across bacteria, patients, and geographical locations, so what appears to be a single global decline is actually the accumulation of many smaller changes.

This makes antibiotics difficult to manage as a shared resource. Fisheries can impose quotas and grazing land can be subject to rotational management, but there is no equivalent system governing antibiotic use across healthcare systems and individual prescribers. Decisions are made locally, while their consequences may emerge elsewhere, sometimes years later. The person making the decision to prescribe an antibiotic therefore rarely bears the full cost of the resistance that use may contribute to.

Stewardship campaigns often approach unnecessary prescribing as an educational problem, but I would argue that it is also an incentives problem. Consider a doctor treating a patient whose symptoms could plausibly have a bacterial cause, but are statistically more likely to be viral. The diagnosis is uncertain, yet the consequences of the two possible decisions are asymmetric. If the doctor withholds antibiotics and the patient has a serious bacterial infection, the error may become apparent quickly. The patient deteriorates, and the doctor may face scrutiny for having withheld treatment. If the doctor prescribes antibiotics for a viral infection, the immediate consequence may be negligible. The resistance selected by that prescription is far less visible, and its costs are distributed among future patients.

Asking clinicians to prescribe more cautiously without addressing these incentives therefore requires them to accept an immediate and identifiable risk in exchange for a benefit that may only be realised by someone else years later. It is understandable that this approach has limited impact.

Furthermore, public discussion of stewardship tends to focus heavily on doctors and patients, despite substantial antibiotic use occurring outside healthcare settings. In livestock farming, antibiotics may be used to treat disease, prevent infections or, in some countries, promote growth <sup>3</sup>. Resistant bacteria can subsequently move beyond farms through food, water, and contact with workers. This part of the problem is harder to visualise. Most people can picture a patient asking a doctor for antibiotics; far fewer will ever see antibiotics being used routinely on a farm. The difference in visibility can make some sources of antibiotic use appear more significant than others, even though resistance can emerge wherever antibiotics exert selective pressure.

A further problem exists upstream of prescribing. Effective stewardship can make antibiotics commercially unattractive to pharmaceutical companies. From a public health perspective, a successfully stewarded antibiotic is one that is used as little as possible and reserved for situations in which it is genuinely needed. For a company that has invested hundreds of millions in its development, however, limited use translates into limited sales and a poor return on investment. Several pharmaceutical companies have withdrawn from antibiotic development over the past two decades, while some smaller companies have struggled financially even after bringing new antibiotics to market <sup>4</sup>. The conflict is straightforward: public health requires new antibiotics to be available while also requiring them to be used sparingly, whereas pharmaceutical companies need sufficient revenue to recover the costs of developing them.

Improving prescribing practices alone therefore cannot resolve the problem. The economic incentives surrounding antibiotic development also need to change.

Most public health interventions are effective partly because they offer an immediate personal benefit. Vaccination reduces an individual's risk of infection, while wearing a seatbelt increases the chance of surviving a crash. The collective benefit is important, but people are often more willing to change their behaviour when they can identify what they themselves stand to gain.

Stewardship offers no comparable immediate benefit. If you decline an antibiotic for an infection that your body could have overcome without treatment, there may be little obvious reward. The benefit may arrive years later, to people you will never meet, and you will never see the outcome. Stewardship therefore asks people to accept a cost or inconvenience now for a benefit that is distant and collectively shared.

This places antibiotic stewardship within a much older question: what do we owe to people who do not yet exist?

Intergenerational justice asks whether people alive today have obligations to those who will inherit the consequences of our decisions. Climate change is perhaps the most familiar example, but antibiotic resistance raises a similar question. Someone born 30 years from now may develop an infection that today's antibiotics can no longer treat. They will have had no influence over how those drugs were prescribed, yet they may live with the consequences.

That future is not predetermined. New antibiotics may be developed, diagnostic technologies may change prescribing practices, and resistance patterns may shift in ways we cannot predict. Nevertheless, we routinely make decisions under uncertainty, including decisions that affect the options available to people who cannot participate in them. The future patient will probably never know that an unnecessary prescription was refused on their behalf. They will simply encounter a treatment that remains effective when they need it. Stewardship is therefore partly an attempt to preserve that option for someone who cannot ask us to preserve it.

This does not make awareness campaigns irrelevant. Education and clear guidelines can reduce unnecessary prescribing, particularly in straightforward cases where antibiotics are clearly inappropriate. However, changing individual behaviour addresses only part of the problem. A GP must still weigh the immediate risk of withholding treatment against a benefit that may not be realised for years, while a pharmaceutical company has limited financial incentive to develop a drug that public health policy subsequently asks clinicians to use sparingly. The incentives are therefore poorly aligned with the outcome that everyone claims to want: antibiotics that remain effective and available when they are genuinely needed.

This is what makes antibiotic stewardship so difficult. We are asking people to conserve a resource they cannot see, whose depletion is uneven, and whose consequences may fall on someone else many years later. If antibiotic effectiveness is understood as a commons, protecting it cannot depend on individual restraint alone -- the systems surrounding antibiotics must also create incentives that make preserving their effectiveness worthwhile.
