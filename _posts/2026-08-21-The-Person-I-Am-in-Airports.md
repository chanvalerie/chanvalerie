## The Person I Am in Airports

I’m writing this from Gatwick Airport, waiting to board a flight to Jakarta.

There is something strange about airports when you travel between two lives. They are supposed to be places you pass through, but I have spent enough time in them to feel like they are their own kind of home. You arrive as one version of yourself and, somewhere between security and the departure gate, begin becoming another. At Gatwick, I am still very much the person I have become in England, but I am already thinking about who I will be when I arrive. For a few hours, both versions of my life exist at the same time.

I notice it most when I’m flying to Indonesia. In England, I am used to doing things for myself. I make my own plans, decide where I go and what I spend my money on, and can decide to fly somewhere for the weekend just because I feel like it. There is a particular freedom in having a life that is entirely your own, and after years of living this way, I have become very comfortable with it. Then I get on a plane to Jakarta and, somewhere over the Indian Ocean, that independence starts to loosen.

I know what it will feel like when I arrive. My mum will be waiting for me at the airport with a bag full of my favourite snacks, and by the time I get home I will probably already have been asked whether I am hungry, regardless of how recently I ate. For a little while, I become somebody’s daughter again rather than just myself. There is something comforting about how easily it happens, especially because it reminds me that this version of me has not disappeared just because I have spent years living somewhere else.

For a few weeks every year, I become the person I was for the first 17 years of my life. I can spend 11 months in England getting used to making my own decisions, then return to Indonesia and find myself slipping back into routines I had almost forgotten were routines. I don’t consciously decide to become that person again; being around the people who have known me since I was a child seems to bring her back on its own.

At the same time, I know I have changed. My family sees the life I have built in England mostly through the stories I tell them, while I have lived all of it. Coming back can therefore feel like bringing two timelines together: they remember the person who left at 17, while I have spent the years since becoming someone slightly different. Yet being there makes that difference feel smaller. I can still fall into the same habits and feel completely at ease in a life I no longer live every day.

This has made me less certain about what I mean when I say I miss home. I know what I miss, but I don’t always know where to place it. Sometimes it is my family, sometimes it is the familiarity of being somewhere that has known me for most of my life, and sometimes it is simply the feeling of being understood without having to explain myself. England is where I have built my adult life, while Indonesia holds the first 17 years of it. When I am in one, I inevitably think about the other.

The strange thing about leaving is that life continues on both sides of the distance. My parents get older while I am away. My baby cousin gets taller. There are family stories I wasn’t there for and ordinary evenings I never knew happened. At the same time, I am building a life that my family only ever sees in pieces. Neither life is waiting for the other to catch up.

Maybe that is why I have stopped thinking of myself as belonging entirely to one place. The person I was at 17 did not disappear when I left, but neither did she remain unchanged. The person I have become since then grew out of her, and I carry both histories with me.

That is what I like about airports. They are one of the few places where I can exist between those histories without having to choose. Sitting at Gatwick, I am still surrounded by the life I have built in England, while already anticipating the person I will become when I arrive in Jakarta.

The flight will take around 18 hours. Somewhere during those hours, I will cross a line on a map, but I don’t think that is really what I’m travelling between. Perhaps I am travelling between different parts of my own history, which is why I can never quite answer where home is when I am asked. I know the places that have made me who I am. I’m just not sure any one of them is enough to contain all of me.

For now, I’m waiting at the gate.
