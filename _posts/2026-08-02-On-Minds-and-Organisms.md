# On Minds and Organisms

We tend to recognise intelligence through what an organism manages to do. When an animal finds its way through a maze, we infer that it has processed information about its surroundings and adjusted its behaviour accordingly. *Physarum polycephalum*, a single-celled organism without a brain or nervous system, can also navigate mazes, respond to changing conditions and form efficient networks connecting sources of food. Its behaviour resembles problem-solving, although the biological processes producing it differ considerably from those we associate with thought.

In a 2000 study, Nakagaki et al. placed *Physarum* in a maze with food sources positioned at its exits. The organism extended its network through the available passages, eventually establishing connections between the food sources and withdrawing from routes that did not contribute to the final connection. What remained was an efficient path through the maze.<sup>1</sup> No central structure compared possible routes or selected the shortest one; the organism's body changed as it explored, with growth and withdrawal shaping the network over time. A decade later, Tero et al. used *Physarum* to model the Tokyo railway network, arranging food sources to represent urban centres and allowing the organism to develop connections between them. Its resulting network shared several features with the existing railway system, including efficient connections between important locations and a degree of redundancy <sup>2</sup>. The slime mould had not reproduced the decisions of human planners. A biological system following local rules of growth and adaptation had produced a network with some of the same useful properties as one designed by people.

A thermostat also responds to its surroundings and adjusts its behaviour according to information it receives. A change in temperature triggers a response that alters the conditions being measured, yet we would not  describe the thermostat as intelligent. The fact that a system produces a useful outcome tells us nothing about whether it thinks, and complexity alone cannot settle the question either. *Physarum* is a more involved case because its network grows, contracts, and redistributes resources in response to local conditions, with changes to its own structure influencing how it responds next. Its body participates in the process rather than simply carrying out instructions formed elsewhere. This has made slime moulds relevant to research on embodied cognition, which examines how cognitive processes depend on the body and its interactions with the environment. The brain remains central to human thought, but adaptive behaviour in other organisms invites us to examine how much of what we call cognition depends on the particular machinery of a nervous system.

In 2008, Saigusa et al. reported that *Physarum* altered its behaviour after repeated exposure to unfavourable conditions at regular intervals. When an expected interruption failed to occur, the organism's activity still reflected the timing of previous interruptions, although this response weakened over time when the interruptions stopped. The researchers interpreted the findings as evidence that the organism could anticipate periodic events based on its environmental history <sup>3</sup>. There was no nervous system storing an account of previous events, yet earlier conditions influenced the organism's response to a later situation. We usually associate memory with the retention and retrieval of information about the past. In *Physarum*, the relevant information appears in changes to the organism's physiological state and activity. Calling this memory emphasises how previous conditions shape subsequent behaviour, though it does not establish that the organism recalls an event or consciously expects it to happen again. The experiment provides evidence of behavioural anticipation; whether the organism experiences the future as something expected remains unknown.

We infer consciousness in other people partly because they share our biology and can communicate their experiences. In other animals, we can draw on their nervous systems, behaviour and evolutionary relationships with us. A single-celled organism offers fewer familiar points of comparison. The absence of a brain does not prove that experience is impossible, but adaptive behaviour alone cannot establish that experience exists. The mechanisms producing a response and what, if anything, that response feels like are separate questions, and the available evidence speaks much more directly to the former.

The meaning of intelligence depends on which features of behaviour we choose to include. If the term refers to the ability to use information, adapt to changing conditions and solve problems, *Physarum* has a reasonable claim to it. If intelligence requires internal representations, deliberate reasoning or conscious thought resembling our own, the evidence offers less support. Neither definition emerges automatically from the experiments. We establish the criteria, then assess the organism against them. Calling something intelligent resembles calling something cheap: the price of an object is measurable, but whether it counts as cheap depends on what it is being compared with and what someone is willing to pay. Intelligence is harder to quantify, and its criteria are more contested, but the label likewise depends on the standards we bring to the evidence. It can describe an observable capacity for adaptive behaviour without settling whether that behaviour involves thought or experience.

*Physarum polycephalum* navigates mazes, reorganises its network in response to food and retains the effects of earlier conditions in its subsequent behaviour. Biological mechanisms account for much of what researchers observe, yet the word *intelligence* continues to carry assumptions about the kinds of systems capable of producing such behaviour. A slime mould offers no direct account of its own experience, and its behaviour cannot settle whether it has one. What it does reveal is how readily we treat the familiar arrangement of a human mind as the standard against which every other form of adaptive behaviour must be judged.
