# On Minds and Organisms

We tend to recognise intelligence through what an organism can do. When an animal finds its way through a maze, we infer that it has processed information about its surroundings and adjusted its behaviour accordingly. *Physarum polycephalum*, commonly known as a slime mould, is a single-celled organism that forms a spreading network of interconnected tubes rather than a body organised around a brain or nervous system. The tubes continually change in thickness, transporting nutrients through the organism as it grows across surfaces in search of food. Despite lacking neurons, *Physarum* can navigate mazes, reorganise its network in response to its surroundings, and form efficient connections between food sources. Its behaviour resembles problem-solving, although the biological processes producing it do not align with what we usually associate with thought.

In a 2000 study, Nakagaki et al. placed *Physarum* in a maze with food sources positioned at its exits. The organism extended its network through the available passages, eventually establishing connections between the food sources and withdrawing from routes that did not contribute to the final connection. What remained was an efficient path through the maze.<sup>1</sup> No central structure compared possible routes or selected the shortest one; the organism's body changed as it explored, with growth and withdrawal shaping the network over time. A decade later, Tero et al. used *Physarum* to model the Tokyo railway network, arranging food sources to represent urban centres and allowing the organism to develop connections between them. Its resulting network shared several features with the existing railway system, including efficient connections between important locations and a degree of redundancy <sup>2</sup>. The slime mould had not reproduced the decisions of human planners. Instead, a biological system following local rules of growth and adaptation had produced a network with some of the same useful properties as one designed by people.

A thermostat also responds to its surroundings and adjusts its behaviour according to information it receives. A change in temperature triggers a response that alters the conditions being measured, yet we would not  describe the thermostat as intelligent. Suppose it could also retain information about previous temperatures and use that history to anticipate changes. Would that be enough to make it intelligent? If not, what would still be missing? The fact that a system produces a useful outcome tells us nothing about whether it thinks, and complexity alone also cannot settle the question. *Physarum* also responds to its surroundings, and its previous experiences influence its subsequent behaviour. Its body changes as it grows, contracts, and redistributes resources, with those changes affecting how it responds next. Where, then, do we draw the line between a system that responds to its environment and one we consider capable of intelligence?

In 2008, Saigusa et al. reported that *Physarum* altered its behaviour after repeated exposure to unfavourable conditions at regular intervals. When an expected interruption failed to occur, the organism's activity still reflected the timing of previous interruptions, although this response weakened over time when the interruptions stopped. The researchers interpreted these findings as evidence that the organism could anticipate periodic events based on its environmental history.<sup>3</sup> Earlier conditions had influenced how it behaved later, without a nervous system storing an account of what had happened. Calling this memory describes how past conditions shape subsequent behaviour, without implying that the organism consciously recalls an event.

The thermostat thought experiment now becomes harder to dismiss. A system that adjusts its responses according to past conditions has something more than a fixed reaction to a present stimulus, but it remains unclear whether this difference is enough to warrant the word intelligence. We might ask how much information the system retains, how flexibly it uses that information or how successfully it adapts when circumstances change. Each criterion draws the boundary in a different place. The same behaviour might count as intelligent under one definition and as a sophisticated form of regulation under another. Like calling something cheap, the judgement depends on a standard of comparison, although intelligence has no single unit of measurement against which we can settle the question.

We infer consciousness in other people because we share their biology and they can describe their experiences to us. With other animals, their nervous systems, behaviour and evolutionary relationships with us give us further grounds for comparison. *Physarum* sits outside these familiar points of reference: it has no brain, and its behaviour offers little insight into what, if anything, it experiences. We can study how it responds to its surroundings in considerable detail, but that knowledge does not tell us whether those responses are accompanied by subjective experience. An organism might meet a behavioural definition of intelligence without there being any evidence that it experiences the world as we do.

Perhaps the thermostat would need more than memory. Perhaps it would need to learn from its mistakes, change its strategy or apply what it has learned in circumstances it has never encountered. Yet each addition shifts the boundary rather than explaining exactly where it lies. A sufficiently elaborate machine could satisfy many of the criteria we associate with intelligence, while an organism such as *Physarum* meets some of them through the changing structure of its own body. We are left deciding which capacities are important, and why. The question is no longer simply whether a slime mould is intelligent, but rather it is what we believe intelligence must involve before we are willing to recognise it.
