## Wrath

*Fiction. Partly inspired by life.*
___

Although I loved the sounds they could produce, I truly hated piano keys. They were too close together, too easy to miss. I could be wholly absorbed in a piece, my shoulders rising and falling with it, when a stray key would get in the way and the noise of an error would shatter any delusion of natural ability. I hated those lost keys almost as much as I hated the blunt instruments called fingers, but I always blamed the designer of the piano more than my own hands. Why put the keys so close together? A gap here or there, and a stray finger might not find its error so easily. No — that would make learning too easy. Instead he built it so that girls like I could become the easy prey of tutors who enjoyed our failing.

I often wonder what turns an ordinary man into a hateful teacher. It seems too simple to believe that every cruel man one meets in a schoolroom is cruel in every part of his life. These men have wives, who must once have loved them. They have children who, presumably, love them still. They have dressed and fed those children as infants. I refuse to believe a person can be so entirely without feeling that they never once knew joy in caring for something small. And yet this was the kind of man I was given to learn from.

My tutor was vicious. Unbecoming to look at — a small round face, short legs — but, infuriatingly, a brilliant pianist. I envied how easily he played Bach and Vivaldi. His timing was impeccable. Mine was always wrong. My posture was always slouched, my hands were always sloped. Even though I was better than anyone I knew, it was his little mission to ensure my concert hall aspirations were driven firmly out of my mind. What disturbs me most in memory is that he seemed to enjoy the punishing of me — that sardonic little smile, the sweat on his forehead as he leaned over the lid before a scolding, reciting my faults as if reading from a list. There was no warmth in his teaching, no gentleness. He knew that I looked up to him, wishing every time to notice a second of approval or appreciation for my efforts. When I looked at him, he knew how much I wanted the immense honour of his praise. I do not think I really ever received any.

One evening I asked my parents if I might perform for them — a small concert of my own, in our music room, to show them how far I had come. I wanted my father, more than anyone, to see that a life spent bringing the great composers to life was not a foolish thing to want. I practised a movement of one of Beethoven's symphonies, far above my level, evening after evening. On the day of the performance, I had a lesson first. Afterward, I asked my tutor to listen to the piece I had been preparing. I remember believing that I had played it better than I had before. I have never forgotten what he said to me when I came to the end. 

*"That was a disgrace. You will never be good enough to play Beethoven."*

It was needless — spoken as though he were St. Peter of the musical heaven, deciding at the gate who deserved a place among the elite, by no method but his own preference. He fancied himself among them, though a family tutor was the only post he'd ever held. If he, the great musician, had never risen past teaching a child in her parlour, how could he allow that child to rise past him?

It hardly mattered that he succeeded in shattering my confidence. I played well that evening – mother thoroughly enjoyed my performance. She cried at the end. But it changed nothing in my father, who showed no pride and wasted no time telling me so — a smirk of condescension, an accusation of conceit for trying to move him without his leave. I was worth no more than the wave of a hand and the turn of a heel. I had expected as much, and still some part of me had hoped that music might touch whatever goodness was left in him. Father made it very clear what my relationship to the piano was to be: I was to learn to play just enough so I could teach my sons to compose and conduct. I was never to flourish or reinterpret; if anything I played were to have even a tone of creativity, I deserved to be castigated. 

It would have made my life a much more monumental story to have disregarded this decree and became the success he never permitted, that my name found its way beside the composers I once believed the finest minds alive. I could have died an inspiration to women and girls all across the globe for my defiance of the criticism of men and for my unerring passion to pursue the talents I was born with. My story would have been of the utmost empowerment. I would have overturned this country’s obsession with the belittlement of my kind, my *species*. 

My life did not unfold that way. What it has contained instead is a long, ordinary resentment of the arrangement I was born into, and the same powerlessness every woman I know carries to change it. I cannot change how I am seen. I could not, in the end, change even the circumstances of my own marriage. I am to wear this as though it were a medal for enduring it — as if endurance were the whole of what a woman is asked to be.

Even at the Academy in London I met the same judgment I thought I had left behind. I had imagined the city might bring a more generous kind of scrutiny. Instead the only welcome I found there was thin and grudging. Men who feared my progress made certain of my failure to protect their own — averting their eyes in corridors, offering polite, uninterested applause, declining, when asked, to work alongside me.

The one exception was the man who became my husband. As a young man he was short, with heavy arms, and something in his manner seemed either gentle or simply still — I could not always tell which. I believed, when we met, that it was his devotion to music, and nothing else, that kept him from judging me the way the others did; that he cared too much for the work to spare thought for anything so ordinary as what sex I happened to be. His indifference to the question felt, at the time, like consent. Any smile he gave me, over some piece of mine he liked, woke something in me I had never felt before.

What I felt for him was less the happiness itself than the safety of it. I knew his frustrations because I had lived beside my own. I understood his hunger to succeed because it was also mine. He knew music the way my father had — but where my father's knowledge had only ever been used against me, his felt like an invitation. He spoke in staves the way other men spoke in ordinary language, and I saw in him a talent no one had ever troubled to see in me. I told myself it was my duty, as the woman who loved him, to help him become what I could not.

The years since have acquainted me better with the man I married. His genius, I still believe, is real. It is also volatile, and demands far more than it gives. His hair has thinned as steadily as his patience with me. I wonder now whether the way I read him, in those early years, said more about my own need to be understood than about anything true in him. He does not, I think, care little for people in general — only that I have become useful to him chiefly as a comfort, something that clears the way for his work rather than something that shares in it.

___


I feel a pang of hunger and realise, standing, that I have been sitting at our dining table for some time — my hand sore and pale from gripping its edge while I remembered.

I find my husband in his study.

"My love, at what time would you like to eat tonight?"

"Pardon?"

"I am hungry. I wondered when you might be, so I could arrange dinner to match."

"Is this a joke?"

"Should I come back later, darling?"

He is perched at the edge of his stool, marking sheet music by candlelight. He turns, pushes back what used to be hair, and thrusts his spectacles at me as he speaks.

"What do you imagine I do in here, all day and night? Do you take me for some fool scribbling stories, or daubing at pretty landscapes like a child fascinated by colour? I am composing one of the great symphonies of this age — its complexity will be beyond you — and still you stand there, interrupting, because you lack the sense to feed yourself without instruction."

I have learned to weather these storms without visibly shaking, though my eyes still sting and my legs still tremble. I know he sees only a stubborn, impatient woman standing before him. I should weep. I should beg his forgiveness for the intrusion. I do neither. I only stand there, and I suppose to him that looks like insolence.

"Why do you keep looking at me?" he presses. "Do I inspire such awe you cannot look away? Or is it insult you mean? Is there ink on my cheek — is that it?"

He gathers a sheet of blank paper from the floor and rubs it hard against his own face.

"There. Is the ink gone? Can you hear how the paper scrapes? I have given myself a rash for your benefit — all because you saw a mark on my cheek and could not let it lie."

He throws the papers at me. Their edges catch sharp against my skin. He comes close, his voice dropping to something quieter and worse.

"In this room the greatest music this world will hear is being written, and what does its author's wife do? Interrupt him to ask a question any woman with sense could have answered herself. She thinks I've failed already. She thinks the symphony is beneath her notice — jealous, I suppose, that she was never given the tutoring I was. Stupid woman."

He strikes me lightly across the head, more contempt than force, and I understand I have been dismissed.

___


"Stop looking at me. Cease, woman." He shoulders past me through our own front door.

The carriage ride home passed through every stage of his anger — first a silence so total that even the creak of a wheel felt dangerous, then a spitting, relentless string of condemnations that no comfort of mine could soften, then a strange collapse into misery that let me, finally, put a hand through his hair and murmur something kind. Now, in the hall, the anger has returned, worse than before. He beats the wall with his fist. I am paralysed by it — not frightened exactly, or not only that, but emptied of any sense that I might affect what happens next.

"My love," I try, coming in from the cold, "you did all that could be done tonight. They saw how much you cared, and knew it was more than any orchestra of theirs could manage."

It had not, in truth, gone well. Any listener who understood music could hear that he had asked more of his players than they could give — the trumpets never allowed to breathe, the violas driven past what their bows could manage, melodies begun and abandoned before they could resolve into anything. He had wanted too much, too fast, certain his audience would forgive complexity that never became coherence.

He turns and looks at me. I am still holding the door.

"You know how they feel, do you? Did they elect you their representative?"

"Of course not. I only meant that your efforts were not in vain."

"Ah. My deepest apologies, then — I am quite consoled. The history books will record it: the greatest composer who ever lived, whose first symphony his own wife pronounced not in vain."

He begins to close the distance between us.

"Tonight was to be the beginning of my career. Instead I am ruined before I have started, and my wife knows it, and plays at not knowing it — the dutiful actress, performing 'devoted wife' for an audience of one. You have known music as long as I have. You know tonight was wretched. You think the symphony mediocre. Here he stands, then — your mediocre husband. Patron saint of mediocrity."

His face is close enough that I can smell the sweat on him. His spectacles sit crooked. His lip trembles with something close to rhythm.

"Say something. I'm right, aren't I. You think me a failure — a worthless man you tolerate with your presence." Spit catches my face as he speaks. "A wife ought to stand by her husband, not scorn him at his lowest. Your conduct disgraces you."

He strikes me across the mouth.

I collapse before I understand that I have been struck. On the floor, I find my lip split, blood gathering in my cupped hands. I feel no pain. Only a strange, total stillness. This man, whose children I have wanted to bear, whose gift I have never stopped admiring, has hit me harder than I believed him capable of.

He hauls me up by the arms before I can gather myself, and something in the force of his grip breaks the stillness — I begin to fight him. He tears the dress he bought me for tonight. We crash against a table; my mother's gift shatters, flowers and water scattering across the floor. He strikes me again to end the struggle, and we say nothing through any of it, only the sounds two bodies make when neither has words left.

Suddenly, he stops.

He stands over me for a second of sweat and regret while I crouch there, still alive with the urge to flee. I look up to see the ugliest contortion of the evening -- his face brewing a concoction of sorrow and shame. The person behind those eyes has returned, his soul no longer enraged. He notices my stare, meets it for a moment, and releases a howl of a sob. Mucus plods down his lips and stretches into a disgusting cobweb of humiliation as his mouth widens and head points upwards to release a childlike scream to the ceiling. His chest heaves at every inhalation to fuel a further yell of misery at which, I presumed, I was to look without comment. His gaze shifts from above down to his hands, which seem swollen from their efforts. Presenting himself his palms, he regards his weapons of choice with great pity. He is calmed by them, fascinated by them, looking left to right and right to left. It is hard for me to determine precisely what has enchanted him about those hands so much. Is it their current condition? Or, what they have done? Or, to whom? Or even, for what cause? 

He turns them to look at the backs, still searching. His nails carry faint scrapings of my skin, stained with my blood. He does not appear startled, only bewildered. His crying has desisted, his misery departed and replaced by curiosity. He looks down upon me, slumped on the floor, disregarding my existence as anything other than his victim, and back towards his hands. With disgust, I realise he is awestruck. 

The disgust curdles into something heavier as the full weight of what has happened settles on me. I am a destitute woman, impoverished for love or an idea. Tears fill my eyes. I am weak, unenviable, and unremarkable. My joy has become vagabond, my spirit sunken below the Earth. I have no recourse for salvation. My lot is to suffer, as it has always been. My miserable life has been to know pain. Mine has been an existence of academic flagellation. I have come to know woe, and even she feels distaste for me. My chest fills with the sound of a thousand oboes, my neck seizes, and I release a low, deathly scream. 

This scream is like no other I have ever produced. It does more than signal to the world that I am in pain. It goes further than expressing the rage I feel at the circumstances that my life has cast upon me. It interrupts more than just the cruel fascination my husband has for his hands. 

It forces from me a passion to resurrect. It stirs the desire I still hold to have shown myself as a woman strong. It refuses to permit that this cruel man who now languishes before me might have shown himself as better just because of his sex. This disgusting man, whose weeping turns his face to the colour of timidity and insignificance, has insulted God and the nature of his species to betray the woman to whom his soul is bound. 

This moment is my clarity. The piety of my cause is my justification. The impotence of my husband is my motivation. Never in my life has there been a moment more urging that I take by the reins the horse of my course and ride into the moonlight. Terrified, I flee. The garden 
path takes me to the life of my dreams.
