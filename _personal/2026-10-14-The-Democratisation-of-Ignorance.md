# The Democratisation of Ignorance

</p>


The democratisation of knowledge has produced a paradox: information has never been more accessible, yet expertise has become easier to dismiss. The internet has weakened the barriers that once restricted access to learning, while also making it easier for confident nonsense to travel at the speed of attention.

For much of history, education and intellectual authority were closely tied to social position. Access to books, universities, and specialist training was unevenly distributed, leaving large sections of the population outside the institutions that shaped public knowledge.

The authority of experts should never place their claims beyond scrutiny, and formal education does not guarantee sound judgement. Institutions have defended mistaken ideas, excluded people from intellectual life, and sometimes become deeply compromised by their own prestige.

A scientific paper can be downloaded from PubMed in seconds, although understanding its methods may require years of training. Reading a medical study can provide enough vocabulary to challenge a doctor, even if one's understanding of the data remains thin.

Anti-intellectualism flourishes in this environment. It rarely presents itself as hostility towards knowledge. Instead, it appeals to common sense, independence, and a refusal to be patronised. Nobody enjoys being talked down to, and many people are right to distrust institutions that speak with confidence while limiting the evidence they are willing to share.

Education can offer a route to understanding, but it also confers status. Academic language and jargon can mark the boundary between those who belong and those who do not. Credentials can become a source of resentment, particularly when they are used to secure social authority rather than to share insight.

Anti-intellectualism can reproduce the hierarchy it claims to oppose. The academic who looks down on the public is replaced by the person who looks down on academics. One appeals to credentials, the other to a kind of folk autonomy that treats expertise as a form of elitism.

&nbsp;

On social media, the way an argument is presented can matter as much as the evidence behind it. A specialist explaining a complicated finding must account for uncertainty and acknowledge the limits of the available evidence. Someone disputing that finding can do so in a tone of unshakable certainty while refusing to absorb any of the technical context.

The performance of scepticism can be persuasive because it demands so little of the person performing it. A perceived inconsistency becomes proof of dishonesty, while an unanswered question is treated as if it were a decisive argument. This style of criticism often functions less as inquiry than as an attempt to discredit the source before the substance is even examined.

This dynamic can also work in a politician's favour, where rejecting expert advice can be presented as evidence of independence from an establishment that ordinary people have good reason to distrust.

&nbsp;

Modern societies depend on specialised knowledge that most people cannot independently verify. Few people have the training required to evaluate clinical trials or assess the assumptions behind ecological models or complex financial systems.

A patient deserves to be heard by their doctor. Their experience may reveal something the doctor has overlooked, and their questions may expose weaknesses in a diagnosis. Yet listening to a patient is not the same as accepting their interpretation of the science. Expertise does not erase lived experience; it places that experience in conversation with evidence.

Suspicion towards intellectual authority can shape how people respond to being wrong. Admitting ignorance can threaten a person's sense of competence, particularly when knowledge has become tied to status and self-respect.

This attitude reflects a confusion between the right to hold an opinion and the quality of the reasoning behind it. Everyone should be free to express their views without needing permission from a credentialed elite. But not every opinion deserves equal weight simply because it is sincerely held.

&nbsp;

The democratisation of knowledge cannot eliminate intellectual inequality. People will continue to differ in what they know, how carefully they reason, and how well they understand the evidence available to them.

Universal access can encourage the expectation that knowledge should require no effort at all. Information is expected to be immediately comprehensible, expertise is expected to justify itself in plain language, and any lack of fluency is treated as evidence of bad faith rather than a sign of real intellectual work.

Ignorance is an ordinary condition of human life, yet acknowledging it can feel like surrendering ground to someone else. Refusing to admit what we do not know may protect our sense of competence, but it often transforms uncertainty into self-righteousness.

A democratic intellectual culture must make room for people to challenge established knowledge without treating ignorance as a source of pride. The aim is not to restore the old deference but to preserve the possibility that anyone can learn and revise their beliefs in the light of better reasons.

The democratisation of ignorance begins when the freedom to question becomes a reason to stop listening. Recovering the willingness to learn from people whose knowledge exceeds our own is part of what a serious democracy requires.
