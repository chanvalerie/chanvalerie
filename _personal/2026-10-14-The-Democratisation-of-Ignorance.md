# The Democratisation of Ignorance

The democratisation of knowledge has produced a paradox: information has never been more accessible, yet expertise has become easier to dismiss. The internet has weakened the barriers that once restricted access to intellectual life, so that questioning an established idea no longer requires the permission of a university or a professional body. Anyone can now publish an argument or claim authority on a subject, but establishing whether the claim is sound takes as much work as it ever did, and access to information has outpaced the ability to evaluate it.

The title of this essay describes something much older than the internet. In 1980, Isaac Asimov described a strain of anti-intellectualism running through American life that is fed by the notion that democracy means "my ignorance is just as good as your knowledge."<sup>1</sup> The sentence conflates two different ideas: equality, which concerns the equal standing people owe one another and the right of everyone to have a voice in deciding how society is run, regardless of their education; and knowledge, which concerns what there is reason to believe and which is distributed far less evenly. Everyone has an equal right to participate in democratic life, but that does not make every claim equally credible. The democratisation of ignorance begins when equality of voice is taken to mean equality of knowledge, and the right to express an opinion becomes a claim to have it treated as true.

The idea Asimov described had already been examined decades earlier. In his 1963 study of American life, Richard Hofstadter defined anti-intellectualism as resentment and suspicion of intellectual life and the people associated with it. He traced its roots to forces that had little to do with stupidity, including an evangelical religion that valued feeling over learning and a democratic temperament suspicious of claims to superior standing.<sup>2</sup> Much of the resentment was earned, since experts did look down on people and the learned professions did guard their gates.

What has changed since Hofstadter is the cost of acting on that resentment, because dismissing the experts once required a pulpit or a newspaper; now, a phone is enough. Platforms reward statements that provoke a reaction, and the qualities that make specialist advice reliable, such as stated uncertainty and revision as evidence arrives, make it less compelling than a confident dismissal from someone under no obligation to qualify anything. Guidance that changes can be presented as proof of dishonesty, and an open question as proof that nobody knows anything. The rejection of expert opinion can attract public support long before anyone has examined the evidence behind it, giving it a credibility it has not earned.

Scepticism deserves defending, because some of the most impactful challenges to expertise have come from outsiders. After Chernobyl in 1986, the British government restricted the movement and slaughter of sheep on the Cumbrian fells, and scientists expected the restrictions to last about three weeks, having assumed that the radioactive caesium would be fixed in the soil as it is in clay.<sup>3</sup> The fells are covered in acidic upland peat instead. The caesium stayed available to grazing animals, and the last Cumbrian controls were lifted only in 2012. Farmers who knew the land and the way their sheep ranged over it had reason to doubt the three-week estimate and were given little hearing, and the sociologist Brian Wynne, who studied the episode, argued that the dismissal of local knowledge did lasting damage to trust in the scientists. The AIDS activists of the 1980s provide a second case: they taught themselves enough virology and trial design to argue with researchers on their own ground, and they changed how trials were run.<sup>4</sup>

What separates these cases from anti-intellectualism is the target of the challenge. The farmers and the activists went after a claim and brought evidence the experts lacked, whereas anti-intellectualism goes after the standing of the person making the claim and asks who the experts think they are, a question that no measurement can answer. A challenge to a claim can be answered with evidence, but a challenge to someone's right to speak cannot be resolved on those terms, which helps explain why it persists so readily and costs so little to make.

The MMR scare began in 1998, when *The Lancet* published a study of twelve children that floated a link between the vaccine and autism.<sup>5</sup> The study had no control group and could say nothing about cause, and far larger studies later found no association, among them a Danish cohort of more than 650,000 children.<sup>6</sup> The paper was retracted and its lead author struck off. His credential had been real, since he was a doctor publishing in a leading journal, and the claim failed because it lacked evidence.

The retraction was then read by his supporters as proof of suppression, which is the move that defines the pattern: once distrust of the institution is the starting point, a correction counts as evidence for the claim it corrects. The anecdotes carried real force, since parents who watched a child change after a vaccination wanted an explanation, and the first signs of autism commonly become noticeable at about the age at which the vaccine is given, so that timing alone can look like cause to anyone without the comparison data.

Politicians have also found ways to turn public distrust of expertise to their advantage.  In a Sky News interview during the 2016 referendum campaign, Michael Gove, challenged over economists' warnings about leaving the European Union, said that people in this country had had enough of experts from "organisations with acronyms" who claimed to know best and had been consistently wrong.<sup>7</sup> The line found an audience because distrust of institutions has a history behind it, and institutions that conceal their mistakes forfeit trust that repeated requests to trust them cannot restore. The same move works whether or not the experts happen to be right, since a politician only has to persuade an audience that the expert has an agenda for the argument to be over before any evidence arrives.

Part of the appeal lies in the status attached to academic language, which has long marked who belongs. People who have been talked down to have good reason to enjoy seeing that status punctured. Rejecting expertise feels like an assertion of equality, yet it often reproduces the hierarchy it claims to oppose, with the academic who looks down on the public replaced by a public that looks down on academics, and each side spends more effort on who deserves to speak than on whether the argument holds. Admitting ignorance is difficult in a culture where confidence is easily mistaken for competence. Changing one's mind can look like losing, while conceding that someone else knows more can feel like surrendering status. Rejecting their expertise, by contrast, allows someone to hold on to it.

The desire to preserve one's independence runs up against a practical problem: modern life depends on knowledge most people cannot verify for themselves. Patients rely on trial data they will never see and passengers on calculations they could not follow.<sup>8</sup> Trust in expertise is the price of a world this complicated, and experts have to keep earning it, but a person who stops trusting the doctor still has to believe someone about the vaccine, and that someone is usually whoever sounds most certain and answers to nobody. The independence on offer in anti-intellectualism therefore tends to hand people over to sources with less accountability than the ones it rejected.

A workable response has to keep the gates open while allowing that claims differ in how much they deserve to be believed. Equal standing in public decisions can coexist with unequal weight for claims, and a patient who notices a symptom the doctor missed has contributed something real without having established its cause. Experts owe the public their working and a ready admission of error, and the public owes experts the question that every good critic asks, namely what the evidence is and what would change the speaker's mind. Questioning of that kind can go on indefinitely without anyone being asked to believe on authority alone, whereas the democratisation of ignorance names the point at which the freedom to question has become a reason to stop listening.
