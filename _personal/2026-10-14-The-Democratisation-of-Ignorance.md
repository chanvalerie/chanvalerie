# The Democratisation of Ignorance

The democratisation of knowledge has produced a paradox: information has never been more accessible, yet expertise has become easier to dismiss. The internet has weakened the barriers that once restricted access to intellectual life, allowing people to question established ideas without the permission of universities, professional bodies, or political institutions. It has also made it easier to mistake access to information for an understanding of it.

For much of history, education and intellectual authority were closely tied to social position. Access to books, universities, and specialist training was unevenly distributed, leaving large sections of the population dependent on institutions they had little power to challenge. The expansion of public education and the arrival of the internet have changed this relationship, whereby someone without a university degree can now read scientific papers, follow academic debates, and question the conclusions of people who have spent their lives studying a subject.

This is a significant achievement -- the authority of experts should never place their claims beyond scrutiny, and formal education does not guarantee sound judgement. Institutions have defended mistaken ideas, excluded people from intellectual life, and used their reputations to discourage legitimate criticism. Scepticism towards authority has often been necessary for knowledge to advance. However, the ability to challenge expertise has increasingly been confused with the ability to evaluate it.

A scientific paper can be downloaded off PubMed in seconds, although understanding its methods may require years of training. Reading a medical study can provide enough vocabulary to challenge a doctor without providing enough knowledge to assess the strength of the argument. Familiarity with a subject can create a sense of competence that exceeds what has actually been learned.

The internet has expanded access to knowledge while making the appearance of knowledge easier to manufacture. A confident opinion can circulate as easily as a carefully supported conclusion, and the two may reach the same audience without any obvious indication of their different foundations.

Anti-intellectualism flourishes in this environment; it rarely presents itself as hostility towards knowledge. Instead, it appeals to common sense, independence, and the refusal to be patronised. Nobody wants to be told what to think by someone who assumes they are incapable of thinking for themselves. When intellectual authority is associated with social privilege, rejecting it can feel like an assertion of equality.

Education can offer a route to understanding but it also confers status. Academic language and jargon can mark the boundary between those who belong and those who do not. Credentials can become a convenient way to establish superiority, allowing people to dismiss an argument before considering its merits. Someone who has been made to feel intellectually inferior may understandably question the institutions that taught them to feel that way.

The reaction becomes problematic when resentment towards intellectual elitism turns into suspicion of expertise itself. Anti-intellectualism can reproduce the hierarchy it claims to oppose. The academic who looks down on the public is replaced by the person who looks down on academics. One appeals to credentials, the other to common sense. Both can become more absorbed in establishing who deserves to speak than in examining whether an argument is sound.

Social media encourages this contest, where a specialist explaining a complicated finding must account for uncertainty and acknowledge the limits of the available evidence. Someone disputing that finding can attract attention with a confident dismissal. The specialist's caution may be interpreted as evasiveness, while the confidence of their opponent is mistaken for clarity.

The performance of scepticism can be persuasive because it demands so little of the person performing it. A perceived inconsistency becomes proof of dishonesty, and an unanswered question is treated as evidence that an entire field has failed. The sceptic gains credibility by appearing unconvinced, even when no attempt has been made to establish whether the claim under scrutiny is true.

Questioning authority is essential to intellectual life, but questioning without examining the evidence can become a way of avoiding the work that actual criticism requires.

Politics has found a use for this attitude. A politician who rejects expert advice can present the rejection as evidence of independence from an establishment that ordinary people have good reason to distrust. The fact that specialists disagree with them can be somehow framed as proof that they have challenged a system determined to preserve its own power.

Public distrust rarely emerges without cause – plenty of institutions have lost credibility when they make mistakes, conceal information, or fail the people they are supposed to serve. Telling the public to trust them more is unlikely to repair that damage, yet distrust can be exploited by those who have little interest in addressing its causes. A politician does not have to demonstrate that an expert is wrong if they can persuade an audience that the expert is unworthy of belief.

The consequences extend beyond political rhetoric. Modern societies depend on specialised knowledge that most people cannot independently verify. Few citizens have the training required to evaluate clinical trials or assess the assumptions behind economic forecasts. Even those with advanced education must rely on experts outside their own fields. No one can personally establish every fact on which their understanding of the world depends.

This dependence sits awkwardly beside the democratic ideal of equality. Every citizen deserves a voice in decisions that affect their life, regardless of their education or occupation. Yet equal political standing cannot mean that every claim about the world is equally well supported. The right to participate in a debate does not guarantee that everyone is equally equipped to settle every question raised within it.

A patient deserves to be heard by their doctor. Their experience may reveal something the doctor has overlooked, and their questions may expose weaknesses in a diagnosis. Still, the patient and the doctor do not necessarily possess the same understanding of medicine. Treating them with equal respect does not require pretending otherwise.

The same principle applies beyond medicine. Public scrutiny of expertise is essential, particularly when experts influence decisions made on behalf of others. Their conclusions should remain open to challenge, and their methods should withstand examination. Challenges must also be judged according to the evidence supporting them. Otherwise, public accountability becomes a competition in which the most convincing speaker wins.

A culture that treats intellectual authority with suspicion also changes the way people respond to being wrong. Admitting ignorance becomes uncomfortable when it can be interpreted as an admission of inferiority. Changing one's mind can look like weakness, while refusing to concede an argument becomes a sign of strength. Someone who learns from a correction may lose face in public. Someone who refuses to learn can preserve the appearance of certainty indefinitely.

The ability to revise a belief should count as evidence of intellectual seriousness. Yet public debate often rewards consistency even when consistency requires ignoring new evidence. Being right becomes less important than appearing impossible to correct.

This attitude is sustained by a confusion between the right to hold an opinion and the quality of the reasoning behind it. Everyone should be free to express their views without needing permission from an institution or professional class. Their freedom to speak does not settle whether their conclusions are justified. A person's dignity should never depend on their level of education, although the strength of an argument must depend on more than the dignity of the person making it.

Arguments about knowledge often become arguments about status. When an expert dismisses someone as ignorant, the insult can obscure whatever truth the expert was trying to communicate. When the person being corrected refuses to consider the argument, the defence of their dignity can become a defence of their error. Both reactions make it harder to separate the question of who deserves respect from the question of what deserves belief.

The democratisation of knowledge cannot eliminate intellectual inequality. People will continue to differ in what they know, how carefully they reason, and how well they understand the evidence available to them. Opening access to information does not remove the work required to make sense of it.

A more troubling possibility is that universal access has encouraged the belief that knowledge should require no effort at all. Information is expected to be immediately comprehensible, expertise is expected to justify itself in the language of everyday experience, and complexity is often treated as evidence of pretension. When an explanation proves difficult to follow, it becomes easier to suspect the person giving it than to accept the limits of one's own understanding.

This expectation makes intellectual humility unusually difficult. Ignorance is an ordinary condition of human life, yet acknowledging it can feel like surrendering ground to someone else. The refusal to admit what we do not know offers temporary protection from that discomfort. It also leaves us more vulnerable to people who are willing to exploit it.

The promise of democratised knowledge was that more people could take part in intellectual life. That promise becomes hollow if participation comes to mean that every claim deserves equal credibility, regardless of the evidence behind it. A society can reject the idea that knowledge belongs to an elite without pretending that knowledge comes equally to everyone.

The right to question an expert should be unconditional. The expectation that an expert must be wrong simply because they occupy a position of authority is another form of intellectual dogmatism. One demands that authority justify itself; the other decides the answer before the evidence has been examined.

A democratic intellectual culture must allow people to challenge established knowledge without making ignorance a source of pride. It must also allow expertise to command respect without granting experts immunity from criticism. That requires accepting an uncomfortable fact: equality does not mean that everyone knows the same things, and acknowledging someone else's knowledge does not make anyone else less worthy.

The democratisation of ignorance begins when the freedom to question becomes a reason to stop listening. Its remedy cannot be a return to unquestioned authority. It depends on recovering the willingness to be taught, especially by people whose knowledge exceeds our own.
