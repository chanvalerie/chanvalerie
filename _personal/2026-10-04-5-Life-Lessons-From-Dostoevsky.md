&nbsp;

Few writers make the human mind feel quite so contradictory as Fyodor Dostoevsky. Dostoevsky’s own life was marked by political persecution, a death sentence that was commuted at the last moment, and years of imprisonment and forced labour in Siberia. These experiences shaped a body of work concerned with morality, faith, freedom, and suffering. More than a century after his death, his novels continue to resonate with readers, and he remains one of my favourite authors for the way his work challenges me to reconsider what I think I know about people, including myself.

Anyway, here’s what reading Dostoevsky has taught me, for whatever that says about my social life and how well-adjusted I am.

&nbsp;

#### **1. Self-awareness is useless without the willingness to change**
_**Notes From the Underground**_

The narrator in _Notes From the Underground_ spends his days in a St. Petersburg room examining his spite and social awkwardness, tracing his resentment to the insecurities that shape his interactions with others. He understands why he insults people who make him feel inferior, and why even an incidental glance can feel like an attack on his dignity. He can account for his hostility at length, often with an almost painful awareness of his own motives. His relationships, however, continue to suffer from the same behaviour.

The Underground Man seems to take a peculiar satisfaction in understanding his own misery. He examines his resentment so closely that it becomes central to his identity, giving him an explanation for his failures and a reason to distrust the people around him. Even when he recognises the harm caused by his actions, he struggles to imagine himself behaving differently. His self-awareness becomes another way of remaining trapped in the life he claims to despise.

People can fall into a similar pattern. Someone might spend an evening reflecting on why they became defensive during an argument, tracing their reaction to childhood experiences or a fear of rejection. The explanation can bring relief, since it makes their behaviour easier to understand. The next disagreement arrives, and their partner receives the same sharp response. Reflection has taken place, but the person has yet to take responsibility for what they do with that knowledge.

Introspection can become an elaborate form of avoidance. People learn to describe their jealousy, temper or fear of rejection while continuing to inflict those feelings on others. In some cases, understanding a flaw even makes it easier to excuse: the behaviour has an explanation, so there seems to be less urgency to change it. Dostoevsky's narrator illustrates the limits of knowing oneself when that knowledge never leads to a change in conduct. Self-awareness only begins the work. The harder task is acting against the instincts that have shaped one's behaviour for so long.

#### **2. Be suspicious of your own justifications**
_**Crime and Punishment**_

In _Crime and Punishment_, Rodion Raskolnikov develops a theory that divides humanity into ordinary and extraordinary individuals. He argues that exceptional people have the right to transgress conventional morality in pursuit of a greater purpose, invoking Napoleon as an example of someone whose achievements required him to cross boundaries that would condemn an ordinary person. Raskolnikov begins to wonder whether he belongs to this exceptional category, and his theory gradually becomes tied to his plans for the pawnbroker.

His argument gives the murder an intellectual purpose, though his motivations are considerably more personal. He is impoverished, humiliated by his inability to pay his rent and resentful of his own insignificance. He wants to prove that he is capable of acting beyond the restrictions imposed on ordinary people. The murder becomes a test of his identity, while his theory gives him a way to imagine the crime as evidence of his superiority.

His response to the murder exposes the weakness of this reasoning. Fever, panic and guilt overwhelm him, and his attempts to defend what he has done become increasingly desperate. He continues to construct arguments for the crime, even as his condition reveals how deeply it has affected him. The person who imagined himself capable of rising above conventional morality finds himself unable to bear the consequences of doing so.

Raskolnikov's reasoning has parallels in everyday life. During an argument, someone who feels embarrassed or slighted might begin collecting evidence against the person who caused the offence. A careless remark becomes an insult, and a disagreement becomes proof of the other person's poor character. Before long, wounded pride has been recast as a defence of fairness or self-respect. The original reaction disappears beneath an argument that makes the person feel entirely justified.

Intelligence can serve the interests of the ego. People often assume that a well-reasoned argument must reflect a considered judgement, when it can just as easily be an elaborate defence of something they wanted to do anyway. Raskolnikov's theory allows him to avoid confronting the more ordinary emotions behind his ambition: envy, humiliation and the desire to feel superior. His story encourages suspicion towards our own reasoning, particularly when we find ourselves constructing elaborate defences for behaviour that has hurt someone else. The feelings behind an argument can reveal more than the arguments themselves.

#### **3. Never let your pride become more important than your happiness**
_**Notes From the Underground**_

An officer in a tavern once moves the Underground Man aside without acknowledging him. The incident barely registers with the officer, while the Underground Man spends years thinking about how to reclaim his dignity. He eventually saves enough money for a better coat so that he can deliberately collide with the officer on the pavement, hoping to force an acknowledgement of his presence.

When the collision finally happens, the officer simply continues walking, apparently unaware of what the encounter means to the Underground Man. He has spent years preparing for a moment of retaliation against someone who had barely noticed him. His humiliation was real to him, even if the offence itself was imagined. The officer has long since forgotten the incident, while the Underground Man has allowed it to occupy years of his life.

People repeat this pattern after awkward social encounters or frustrating conversations at work. They replay a careless comment long after the other person has forgotten it, imagining the response they should have given or how they could have defended themselves. The exchange continues in their head, sometimes for days. Letting it go can feel like admitting defeat, even when the other person has already moved on.

Pride can be even more damaging in close relationships. After an argument, apologising first or being the one to reach out may feel like admitting to being the more invested partner. Both people wait for the other to make the first move, and affection becomes something neither wants to offer without receiving it first. They may both want reassurance, while their fear of appearing vulnerable keeps them apart.

The Underground Man's relationship with Liza reveals a deeper version of this insecurity. When she sees through his posturing and offers him affection, he cannot accept the vulnerability of being cared for. Her kindness exposes the loneliness beneath his attempts to appear superior, and he responds by humiliating her. He would rather regain a sense of control than risk allowing someone to know him as he really is. His treatment of Liza shows how the desire to protect one's pride can become destructive, particularly when it prevents the possibility of intimacy.

Pride has its place when someone needs to stand up for themselves after being mistreated. It becomes destructive when every disagreement turns into a contest over who cares less. Apologising first or walking away without the last word does not make someone weak. The Underground Man spends years trying to prove that nobody can humiliate him, while his fear of appearing inferior keeps him from forming the connections he wants. A life organised around defending one's pride can leave very little room for happiness.

#### **4. Being kind in a cruel world does not make you an idiot**
_**The Idiot**_

Prince Myshkin arrives in St. Petersburg society with an openness that unsettles the people around him. He speaks honestly, listens to those who mock him and takes an interest in people whose reputations others have already judged. He has little interest in the social manoeuvring that governs much of their behaviour. His willingness to approach people without suspicion leads many to regard him as a fool. They struggle to understand why someone would choose sincerity in a society so preoccupied with status and self-interest.

Myshkin is not unaware of the cruelty around him. He often recognises the suffering behind people's behaviour and responds with compassion rather than ridicule. He does not need to make someone else feel small to establish his own intelligence, nor does he treat every interaction as an opportunity to defend his reputation. His kindness comes from his willingness to see people beyond their social standing or the mistakes they have made.

It is easy to become guarded after being hurt. A failed friendship or an experience of being taken advantage of at work can make someone reluctant to trust again. Sarcasm can begin to look like intelligence, while sincerity gets dismissed as naivety. Over time, expecting the worst from people can become a habit. Even those who approach with good intentions may find themselves treated with suspicion.

Myshkin offers a different way of approaching people. He remains sincere in situations where others resort to mockery or indifference, and he does not regard kindness as something to be embarrassed about. Refusing to respond to cruelty with more cruelty takes courage, particularly when patience is mistaken for weakness. Past experiences can explain why someone becomes guarded, but they need not determine how that person treats everyone they meet afterwards.

Dostoevsky does not present Myshkin as a perfect example of goodness. His difficulty establishing boundaries leads him into other people's conflicts, and his reluctance to make difficult decisions contributes to the suffering around him. He often understands someone's pain without knowing how to help, and his desire to be kind can leave him caught up in situations he cannot resolve. His character also exposes a difficulty with compassion: understanding someone's suffering does not necessarily mean knowing what they need, and an unwillingness to confront harmful behaviour can allow it to continue.

Myshkin's example is worth considering alongside these failures. Kindness requires judgement as well as compassion. It is possible to remain considerate without allowing others to take advantage, and to approach people sincerely while recognising when a relationship has become harmful. A difficult experience may change how readily someone trusts, but it need not turn them into a person who treats everyone with suspicion. The cruelty of others does not have to dictate the kind of person we choose to be.

#### **5. A meaningful life is built through love and responsibility towards others**
_**The Brothers Karamazov**_

In The Brothers Karamazov, Father Zosima observes how easy it is to love humanity in the abstract while finding individual people unbearable. General affection asks very little of its owner. Someone can think of themselves as compassionate without having to deal with another person's needs or shortcomings. The people closest to us present a different experience of love, since their habits and expectations become part of everyday life.

It is easy to imagine oneself as generous when nobody needs anything. The test comes when a family member is irritable, a partner forgets a promise or a colleague asks for help at the end of an exhausting day. Love then becomes something expressed through ordinary behaviour, often when we have other things we would rather be doing. Zosima asks us to look at how we treat the people around us when their needs compete with our own.

Arguments reveal how differently we expect to be treated from how we treat others. We want our frustrations understood immediately, while interpreting another person's mistakes as evidence of poor character. We expect patience when under pressure, then struggle to offer the same consideration when someone else is having a difficult day. These exchanges shape the atmosphere of a relationship. Over time, people learn whether they can turn to us when things go wrong or whether they should expect another criticism.

Alyosha embodies Zosima's understanding of love through his relationships with others. He visits the sick, listens to grieving parents and spends time with children who need someone to take them seriously. His faith finds expression in these encounters. He does not rely on philosophical arguments to demonstrate his beliefs; he puts them into practice through the attention he gives to people around him, including those whose suffering he cannot resolve.

Believing in kindness is different from making the effort to be kind when it costs something. Someone can spend years discussing morality while remaining impatient with their own family. Another person may never articulate a philosophy of love, but will consistently show up when a friend needs support. Their conduct says more about their values than any account they could give of themselves. Beliefs are tested in ordinary situations, when following them requires time or patience and nobody is there to offer praise.

Discussions about a successful life often centre on career achievements, professional recognition and personal ambitions. These are easy to describe and measure, while the obligations that sustain relationships receive less attention. Listening when someone needs to talk, apologising after causing hurt or helping without expecting recognition may seem like small gestures. For the people receiving them, they can be the clearest expressions of care.

Dostoevsky places Alyosha's goodness among people whose needs are demanding and whose lives are difficult. His character suggests that a life guided by principle must include the responsibilities we accept towards others. Love cannot remain an idea directed towards humanity as a whole. It has to be practised with the people who share our lives, including those who test our patience and cannot always give something back. The way we treat those closest to us says far more about our values than the principles we claim to believe in.

___

&nbsp;

What I find most valuable about Dostoevsky is how his novels have made me more conscious of the ways in which I can be my own worst enemy. I overthink when I should act, rationalise when I should be honest, and allow my pride to interfere with things I care about. I spend so much time trying to understand why other people behave the way they do that I sometimes forget to examine my own behaviour with the same scrutiny.

His characters have also made me more sympathetic towards the contradictions that exist in other people. It is easy to judge someone by their worst moments, particularly when those moments have hurt us. It takes greater generosity to recognise that they, too, are navigating their own insecurities and internal conflicts. Understanding this does not absolve anyone of responsibility for their actions. It means resisting the temptation to reduce an entire person to a single experience of them.

The common thread running through these lessons is humility. It takes humility to recognise that I am not always right about myself, that my intentions are not always as noble as I would like to believe, and that other people possess an inner world just as complicated as my own.

Dostoevsky's novels offer few comfortable conclusions about human nature. They remind me that knowing better does not guarantee doing better. Becoming the person I would like to be requires a willingness to examine my own behaviour, including the parts I would prefer to defend.

I have no intention of becoming someone without contradictions. His characters have made me more accepting of the contradictions that come with being human, while reminding me that they do not have to dictate how I live. The goal is to become a little less defensive, a little more honest with oneself, and a little more generous towards others.
