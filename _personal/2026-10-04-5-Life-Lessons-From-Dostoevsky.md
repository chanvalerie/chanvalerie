&nbsp;

Few writers make the human mind feel quite so contradictory as Fyodor Dostoevsky. Dostoevsky’s own life was marked by political persecution, a death sentence that was commuted at the last moment, and years of imprisonment and forced labour in Siberia. These experiences shaped a body of work concerned with morality, faith, freedom, and suffering. More than a century after his death, his novels continue to resonate with readers, and he remains one of my favourite authors for the way his work challenges me to reconsider what I think I know about people, including myself.

Anyway, here’s what reading Dostoevsky has taught me, for whatever that says about my social life and how well-adjusted I am.

___

#### 1. Self-awareness is useless without the willingness to change
_**Notes From the Underground**_

The Underground Man is perhaps one of the most fascinating characters in literature. He is acutely aware of his own shortcomings. He recognises his vanity, resentment, insecurity, and the contradictions that govern his behaviour. He can identify precisely why he does things, often while acknowledging how ridiculous those things are. Yet this extraordinary capacity for introspection does very little to improve his life. If anything, it makes him more miserable.

He spends so much time analysing his own consciousness that he becomes incapable of acting upon it. He anticipates humiliation before it happens, imagines how others perceive him, and constructs elaborate explanations for his own unhappiness. Even the simplest social interactions become intellectual exercises. He would rather spend hours contemplating an action than risk actually taking it.

There is something particularly revealing about the way the Underground Man uses self-awareness. He seems to believe that understanding his own behaviour is equivalent to having some control over it. Yet he repeatedly falls into the same patterns, almost taking pleasure in his ability to recognise his own misery without doing anything to escape it.

This is a trap that extends well beyond the Underground Man. It is easy to believe that identifying an insecurity, understanding its origins, and recognising its influence somehow constitutes progress. But recognising defensiveness does not automatically make someone less defensive. Understanding why someone overthinks does not necessarily stop them from overthinking.

Self-awareness can become a rather sophisticated form of procrastination. People can spend years analysing their shortcomings, tracing them to childhood experiences or past relationships, without ever addressing the behaviour itself. Explanations may provide understanding, but they do not remove responsibility.

There is a point at which introspection becomes another way of avoiding the difficult work of change. Understanding oneself is only useful when that understanding translates into action. Perhaps genuine self-awareness begins when the question shifts from why someone behaves a certain way to what they intend to do about it.
&nbsp; 

#### 2. Be suspicious of your own justifications
_**Crime and Punishment**_

Raskolnikov's theory in Crime and Punishment is one of the most interesting explorations of self-deception in literature. He divides humanity into ordinary and extraordinary individuals, arguing that certain exceptional people possess the right to transgress conventional morality in pursuit of a greater purpose. He invokes figures such as Napoleon to support his argument, convincing himself that some individuals must be willing to step beyond the boundaries imposed upon everyone else.

His theory is intellectually elaborate, but his actual motivations are considerably less noble. Beneath his philosophical reasoning lie poverty, resentment, wounded pride, and a desire to prove that he belongs among the extraordinary.

What makes Raskolnikov so compelling is that he is not entirely ignorant of his own contradictions. He oscillates between intellectual conviction and moral revulsion, repeatedly attempting to reconcile what he believes he ought to feel with what he actually feels. His mind becomes a battlefield between the person he imagines himself to be and the person his actions reveal.

His story exposes an uncomfortable feature of human reasoning: people are often far better at defending their beliefs than questioning where those beliefs came from.

It is surprisingly easy to construct an argument around an emotional reaction and gradually convince oneself that the argument was the reason for the reaction in the first place. Pride becomes principle, resentment becomes a defence of justice, and insecurity becomes an insistence on being treated fairly. The original emotion becomes buried beneath layers of increasingly convincing reasoning.

This is particularly apparent in disagreements. When someone feels hurt, it is natural to focus on the other person's behaviour, identify inconsistencies, and construct a case for why their own reaction is justified. Sometimes that case is entirely valid. Yet the desire to be right can make it difficult to recognise when an argument is also protecting an underlying insecurity.

Intelligence offers little protection against this. In fact, the ability to reason persuasively can make self-deception considerably more sophisticated.

Raskolnikov serves as a reminder to question the motives behind convictions, particularly when those convictions place their owner in a position of moral superiority. Sometimes the most important question is not whether an argument is logically sound, but what the person making it is trying to protect.

#### 3. Never let your pride become more important than your happiness
_**Notes From the Underground**_

If the first lesson concerns being trapped by one's own thoughts, this one concerns being trapped by one's own ego.

The Underground Man is extraordinarily sensitive to humiliation. He remembers perceived slights long after everyone else has forgotten them, becomes consumed by how others perceive him, and frequently sacrifices his own comfort to preserve his dignity.

One particularly absurd example involves an officer who once moved him aside without acknowledging him. The Underground Man becomes so preoccupied with this perceived humiliation that he eventually begins deliberately walking into the officer, simply to demonstrate that he deserves to occupy the same physical space. The officer barely notices him. The encounter, however, occupies the Underground Man's thoughts for years.

It is simultaneously ridiculous and rather sad.

There is something recognisable in the tendency to give other people's opinions an extraordinary amount of power over one's emotional life. A dismissive comment can occupy an entire afternoon. A disagreement can become less about resolving an issue and more about ensuring the other person understands exactly why they were wrong. Resentment can be preserved long after the original grievance has lost any practical significance, simply because letting it go feels too much like admitting defeat.

Pride can disguise itself as self-respect. The two are not always easy to distinguish. Self-respect allows people to recognise when they have been treated poorly and establish boundaries accordingly. Pride, however, can keep them returning to the same arguments, rehearsing the same grievances, and waiting for an acknowledgement that may never come.

There is also a particular cost to allowing pride to govern relationships. Apologising first can feel like surrender. Admitting hurt can feel like exposing a weakness. Extending affection after an argument can feel like giving the other person an advantage. Consequently, people sometimes withhold the very things they want most because they are unwilling to risk appearing vulnerable.

The Underground Man illustrates the absurdity of this arrangement. His need to preserve his dignity repeatedly deprives him of the possibility of genuine connection.

Not every disagreement needs a winner, not every slight requires a response, and not everyone needs to understand or validate another person's perspective. Sometimes the most dignified thing a person can do is stop trying to prove their dignity in the first place.

#### 4. Being kind in a cruel world does not make you an idiot
_**The Idiot**_

Prince Myshkin is perhaps one of Dostoevsky's most unusual protagonists. He enters a society governed by vanity, social competition, manipulation, and self-interest with an almost disarming sincerity. He is compassionate towards people who are cruel to him, sees dignity in those whom society dismisses, and rarely approaches another person with the intention of gaining something from them.

For this, he is frequently regarded as an idiot.

What makes Myshkin so interesting is that his apparent naivety is often mistaken for an absence of intelligence. He is capable of recognising suffering and understanding the emotional complexity of others. He simply refuses to approach people with the same suspicion and cynicism that seem to govern everyone around him.

There is something quite radical about this.

As people grow older, experiences often teach them to become increasingly guarded. Disappointment encourages suspicion, vulnerability begins to feel like a liability, and emotional detachment can become confused with maturity. Eventually, cynicism starts to look like intelligence, while kindness is dismissed as naivety.

But how much of this is actually wisdom, and how much is simply fear dressed up as experience?

There is no particular virtue in becoming unkind simply because other people can be unkind. Nor is there any wisdom in allowing disappointing experiences to determine how every subsequent person is treated. A world in which people respond to cruelty by becoming increasingly cruel themselves is hardly an improvement on the one they are trying to protect themselves from.

However, Myshkin is not an uncomplicated example to follow. His compassion can become almost paralysing. He struggles to establish boundaries, becomes entangled in other people's suffering, and cannot always distinguish between understanding someone's pain and being responsible for relieving it. His goodness does not protect him from the destructive consequences of the world around him.

This introduces an important qualification: kindness should not require the abandonment of self-respect. Understanding someone's suffering does not mean excusing their behaviour. Forgiveness does not require offering someone unlimited opportunities to cause harm. Compassion does not mean accepting every burden placed upon another person.

Perhaps the real challenge is to remain kind without becoming naive, and to remain open-hearted without making oneself entirely defenceless.

There is something admirable about refusing to let the world's cruelty dictate the kind of person one becomes. Being occasionally mistaken for an idiot because of an unwillingness to abandon kindness seems a far better fate than becoming someone who mistakes cruelty for intelligence.

#### 5. A meaningful life is built through love and responsibility towards others.
_**The Brothers Karamazov**_

For all its philosophical arguments about God, morality, suffering, and the nature of existence, The Brothers Karamazov ultimately offers a remarkably practical understanding of how to live.

Through Father Zosima, Dostoevsky presents the idea that people are responsible for one another. His philosophy places particular importance on what he calls active love: a form of love expressed through patience, compassion, and the willingness to take responsibility for others. He contrasts this with the idea of loving humanity in the abstract, which is considerably easier than loving the actual people encountered in everyday life, with all their irritating habits and imperfections.

I find this particularly compelling because it challenges the way goodness is often understood.

It is easy to admire kindness as an abstract virtue. Most people would agree that compassion, patience, and understanding are desirable qualities. It is considerably harder to practise them when someone has upset you, when you are tired, or when extending understanding requires putting aside your own pride.

There is a tendency to want to be understood before making the effort to understand someone else. When hurt, attention naturally turns towards what the other person should have done differently. Their intentions, circumstances, and difficulties become secondary to the immediate desire for acknowledgement. Yet the same consideration that people expect from others is not always extended in return.

Zosima's philosophy asks for something more demanding than simply being nice to people. It asks people to recognise their connection to one another and to take responsibility for the ways they contribute to each other's lives. It requires looking beyond one's own immediate perspective.

This does not mean accepting responsibility for everything another person does, or tolerating mistreatment in the name of compassion. Rather, it means recognising that individual behaviour has consequences, even when intentions are good.

There is also something comforting in the idea that a meaningful life does not have to be defined by extraordinary achievements. It is easy to become preoccupied with fulfilling one's potential, achieving success, or making some significant contribution to the world. In doing so, the ordinary opportunities to be a good person can become easy to overlook.

Perhaps meaning is found in the patience extended to someone having a difficult day, the effort made to understand someone loved, or the kindness shown when there is nothing to gain from it.

Alyosha embodies this philosophy. While Ivan wrestles with the great intellectual and moral contradictions of existence, Alyosha attempts to live through compassion. He does not need to resolve every philosophical problem before deciding that other people deserve his kindness.

Being a good person is less about having the correct philosophy and more about what that philosophy looks like when translated into everyday behaviour.

___

What I find most remarkable about Dostoevsky is that his novels have made me more conscious of the ways in which I can be my own worst enemy. I can overthink when I should act, rationalise when I should be honest, and allow my pride to interfere with things I care about. I can spend so much time trying to understand why other people behave the way they do that I forget to examine my own behaviour with the same scrutiny.

His characters have also made me more sympathetic towards the contradictions that exist in other people. It is easy to judge someone by their worst moments, particularly when those moments have hurt us. It is harder to recognise that they, too, are navigating their own insecurities, fears, and internal conflicts. Understanding this does not require excusing everything people do. It simply requires resisting the temptation to reduce an entire person to a single experience of them.

I suppose the common thread running through these lessons is humility. The humility to recognise that I am not always right about myself, that my intentions are not always as noble as I would like to believe, and that other people possess an inner world just as complicated as my own.

Perhaps this is why I keep returning to Dostoevsky. His books don't offer particularly comfortable conclusions about human nature. They remind me that being a good person is a continuous undertaking, that knowing better does not always mean doing better, and that there is a great deal of work involved in becoming the person I would like to be.

I don't think the objective should be to become someone without contradictions. Dostoevsky's characters suggest that such a person would hardly be human. Perhaps it is enough to become a little less defensive, a little more honest with oneself, and a little more generous towards others.
