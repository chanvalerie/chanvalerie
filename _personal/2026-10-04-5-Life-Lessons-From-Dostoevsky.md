&nbsp;

Few writers make the human mind feel quite so contradictory as Fyodor Dostoevsky. Dostoevsky’s own life was marked by political persecution, a death sentence that was commuted at the last moment, and years of imprisonment and forced labour in Siberia. These experiences shaped a body of work concerned with morality, faith, freedom, and suffering. More than a century after his death, his novels continue to resonate with readers, and he remains one of my favourite authors for the way his work challenges me to reconsider what I think I know about people, including myself.

Anyway, here’s what reading Dostoevsky has taught me, for whatever that says about my social life and how well-adjusted I am.

&nbsp;

#### **1. Self-awareness is useless without the willingness to change**
_**Notes From the Underground**_

Throughout Notes From the Underground, Dostoevsky presents a protagonist whose consciousness has become an obstacle to his own existence. The Underground Man is deeply conscious of his flaws and contradictions, but remains incapable of breaking the patterns that govern his behaviour. His tendency to anticipate humiliation and dwell on imagined grievances colours his perception of the world around him. Ordinary interactions become occasions for elaborate internal deliberation, leaving him increasingly detached from others and unable to act spontaneously. His introspection becomes another source of suffering, and introspection is useless without any willingness to change.

The Underground Man mistakes his ability to recognise his behaviour for an ability to control it. He repeatedly falls into the same patterns, finding satisfaction in recognising his own misery without doing anything to change it. His self-awareness becomes part of the problem, allowing him to observe his own shortcomings without confronting them.

This tendency extends well beyond the Underground Man. Identifying an insecurity or understanding where it originated can create the impression that something has been resolved. Recognising defensiveness does not automatically make someone less defensive. Understanding why someone overthinks does not stop them from doing so.

Self-awareness can become a sophisticated form of procrastination. People spend years analysing their shortcomings, tracing them back to childhood experiences or past relationships, without changing the behaviours they have spent so long examining. Understanding the reasons behind a behaviour can help explain it, but the responsibility for changing it remains.

The Underground Man exposes the limits of introspection when it becomes an end in itself. Understanding oneself requires more than the ability to recognise one's flaws. It requires the willingness to confront them and make different choices.

&nbsp; 

#### **2. Be suspicious of your own justifications**
_**Crime and Punishment**_

In Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov convinces himself that certain individuals possess the right to transgress conventional morality in pursuit of a greater purpose. He divides humanity into ordinary and extraordinary individuals, arguing that exceptional people must sometimes step beyond the boundaries imposed upon everyone else. He invokes figures such as Napoleon to support his argument, using the achievements of great men to justify his own ambitions.

His theory is intellectually elaborate, but his actual motivations are considerably less noble. Beneath his philosophical reasoning lie poverty, resentment, wounded pride, and a desire to prove that he belongs among the extraordinary.

Raskolnikov is not entirely ignorant of his contradictions. He oscillates between intellectual conviction and moral revulsion, repeatedly attempting to reconcile what he believes he ought to feel with what he actually feels. His mind becomes a battlefield between the person he imagines himself to be and the person his actions reveal.

His story exposes an uncomfortable feature of human reasoning: people are often far better at defending their beliefs than questioning where those beliefs originated.

It is easy to construct an argument around an emotional reaction and gradually convince oneself that the argument was the reason for the reaction in the first place. Pride becomes principle, resentment becomes a defence of justice, and insecurity becomes an insistence on being treated fairly. The original emotion disappears beneath layers of increasingly convincing reasoning.

This is particularly apparent in disagreements. When someone feels hurt, attention naturally turns towards the other person's behaviour. We identify inconsistencies, recall previous grievances, and construct a case for why our own reaction is justified. Sometimes that case is entirely valid. The desire to be right can also obscure the insecurity that an argument is protecting.

Intelligence offers little protection against self-deception. The ability to reason persuasively can make it considerably more sophisticated.

Raskolnikov serves as a reminder to question the motives behind our convictions, particularly when those convictions place us in a position of moral superiority. An argument can be logically sound and still serve a deeply personal interest. The motives behind an argument deserve as much scrutiny as the argument itself.

#### **3. Never let your pride become more important than your happiness**
_**Notes From the Underground**_

If the first lesson concerns being trapped by one's own thoughts, this one concerns being trapped by one's own ego.

The Underground Man is extraordinarily sensitive to humiliation. He remembers perceived slights long after everyone else has forgotten them, becomes consumed by how others perceive him, and repeatedly sacrifices his own comfort to preserve his dignity.

One particularly absurd example involves an officer who once moved him aside without acknowledging him. The Underground Man becomes so preoccupied with this perceived humiliation that he eventually begins deliberately walking into the officer, simply to demonstrate that he deserves to occupy the same physical space. The officer barely notices him. The encounter, however, occupies the Underground Man's thoughts for years.

It is simultaneously ridiculous and deeply revealing.

People give other people's opinions extraordinary power over their emotional lives. A dismissive comment can occupy an entire afternoon. A disagreement becomes an exercise in ensuring the other person understands exactly why they were wrong. Resentment survives long after the original grievance has lost its practical significance, sustained by the belief that letting go would amount to admitting defeat.

Pride frequently disguises itself as self-respect. The distinction is important. Self-respect allows people to recognise when they have been treated poorly and establish boundaries accordingly. Pride keeps them returning to the same arguments, rehearsing the same grievances, and waiting for an acknowledgement that may never come.

Pride can also be particularly destructive in relationships. Apologising first feels like surrender. Admitting hurt feels like exposing a weakness. Extending affection after an argument feels like giving the other person an advantage. Consequently, people withhold the very things they want most because they are unwilling to risk appearing vulnerable.

The Underground Man illustrates the cost of this behaviour. His need to preserve his dignity repeatedly deprives him of genuine connection.

Not every disagreement needs a winner. Not every slight requires a response. Not everyone needs to understand or validate our perspective. Sometimes the most dignified thing a person can do is stop trying to prove their dignity in the first place.

#### **4. Being kind in a cruel world does not make you an idiot**
_**The Idiot**_

Prince Myshkin enters a society governed by vanity, social competition, manipulation, and self-interest with an almost disarming sincerity. He is compassionate towards people who are cruel to him, sees dignity in those whom society dismisses, and rarely approaches another person with the intention of gaining something from them.

For this, he is frequently regarded as an idiot.

His apparent naivety is frequently mistaken for an absence of intelligence. Myshkin is capable of recognising suffering and understanding the emotional complexity of others. He simply refuses to approach people with the suspicion and cynicism that govern those around him.

As people grow older, experience often teaches them to become increasingly guarded. Disappointment encourages suspicion, vulnerability begins to feel like a liability, and emotional detachment becomes confused with maturity. Eventually, cynicism starts to look like intelligence, while kindness is dismissed as naivety.

Cynicism is not necessarily evidence of wisdom. Sometimes it is simply the consequence of allowing disappointing experiences to dictate how everyone else is treated.

There is no virtue in becoming unkind because other people can be unkind. Responding to cruelty with further cruelty only perpetuates the behaviour that made the world difficult to begin with.

Myshkin, however, is not an uncomplicated example to follow. His compassion can become almost paralysing. He struggles to establish boundaries, becomes entangled in other people's suffering, and cannot always distinguish between understanding someone's pain and being responsible for relieving it. His goodness does not protect him from the destructive consequences of the world around him.

Kindness requires boundaries. Understanding someone's suffering does not excuse their behaviour. Forgiveness does not require offering someone unlimited opportunities to cause harm. Compassion does not mean accepting every burden placed upon another person.

The challenge is to remain kind without becoming naive, and to remain open-hearted without making oneself entirely defenceless.

Refusing to let the world's cruelty dictate the kind of person one becomes is an act of conviction. Being occasionally mistaken for an idiot because of an unwillingness to abandon kindness seems a far better fate than becoming someone who mistakes cruelty for intelligence.

#### **5. A meaningful life is built through love and responsibility towards others**
_**The Brothers Karamazov**_

For all its philosophical arguments about God, morality, suffering, and the nature of existence, The Brothers Karamazov offers a remarkably practical understanding of how to live.

Through Father Zosima, Dostoevsky presents the idea that people are responsible for one another. His philosophy places particular importance on what he calls active love: a form of love expressed through patience, compassion, and the willingness to take responsibility for others. He contrasts this with loving humanity in the abstract, which is considerably easier than loving the actual people encountered in everyday life, with all their irritating habits and imperfections.

Zosima's philosophy challenges the way goodness is often understood.

It is easy to admire kindness as an abstract virtue. Most people would agree that compassion, patience, and understanding are desirable qualities. Practising them becomes considerably harder when someone has upset you, when you are tired, or when extending understanding requires putting aside your own pride.

People often want to be understood before making the effort to understand someone else. When hurt, attention turns towards what the other person should have done differently. Their intentions, circumstances, and difficulties become secondary to the immediate desire for acknowledgement. The same consideration expected from others is not always extended in return.

Zosima asks people to recognise their connection to one another and take responsibility for the ways they contribute to each other's lives. This requires looking beyond one's own immediate perspective and acknowledging that individual behaviour has consequences, even when intentions are good.

A meaningful life need not be defined by extraordinary achievements. People become preoccupied with fulfilling their potential, achieving success, and making significant contributions to the world. Meanwhile, the ordinary opportunities to be a good person are easily overlooked.

Meaning can be found in the patience extended to someone having a difficult day, the effort made to understand someone loved, or the kindness shown when there is nothing to gain from it.

Alyosha embodies this philosophy. While Ivan wrestles with the great intellectual and moral contradictions of existence, Alyosha attempts to live through compassion. He does not need to resolve every philosophical problem before deciding that other people deserve his kindness.

Being a good person is ultimately measured by what one's philosophy looks like in everyday behaviour.

&nbsp;

What I find most valuable about Dostoevsky is how his novels have made me more conscious of the ways in which I can be my own worst enemy. I overthink when I should act, rationalise when I should be honest, and allow my pride to interfere with things I care about. I spend so much time trying to understand why other people behave the way they do that I sometimes forget to examine my own behaviour with the same scrutiny.

His characters have also made me more sympathetic towards the contradictions that exist in other people. It is easy to judge someone by their worst moments, particularly when those moments have hurt us. It takes greater generosity to recognise that they, too, are navigating their own insecurities, fears, and internal conflicts. Understanding this does not absolve anyone of responsibility for their actions. It simply means resisting the temptation to reduce an entire person to a single experience of them.

The common thread running through these lessons is humility. The humility to recognise that I am not always right about myself, that my intentions are not always as noble as I would like to believe, and that other people possess an inner world just as complicated as my own.

Dostoevsky's novels offer few comfortable conclusions about human nature. They remind me that being a good person requires continuous effort, that knowing better does not guarantee doing better, and that becoming the person I would like to be demands a great deal of honesty.

I have no intention of becoming someone without contradictions. Dostoevsky's characters suggest that contradictions are inseparable from being human. The goal is to become a little less defensive, a little more honest with oneself, and a little more generous towards others.
