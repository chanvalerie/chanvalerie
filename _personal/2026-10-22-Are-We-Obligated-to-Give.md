# Are We Obligated to Give?

For centuries, religious communities treated helping impoverished neighbours as a command and a civic duty, collecting tithes in Christian parishes and *zakat* under Islamic law. In the 19th and 20th centuries, industrialisation shifted this work from parishes and local religious institutions towards philanthropic trusts and secular charities operating on a global scale.

In his 1889 essay *The Gospel of Wealth*, Andrew Carnegie articulated an explicit standard of secular stewardship, asserting that “the man who dies rich dies disgraced". He argued that people who accumulate great wealth bear a responsibility to put it to public use during their lifetimes. His perspective linked traditional religious obligations with modern secular ethics, making the use of private wealth a matter of social accountability. His perspective linked traditional religious obligations with modern secular ethics, establishing surplus wealth as an area of social accountability.<sup>1</sup>

In recent years, observers have noted that individual giving appears to have diminished across many societies. Rising living costs have tightened household budgets, cyinism about institutional overhead, and fatigue from constant digital appeals also make donors hesitate. Yet human suffering across the globe persists, and the resources needed to address it exist in abundance among affluent populations.

In his 1972 essay *Famine, Affluence, and Morality*, Peter Singer invites readers to imagine walking past a shallow pond and seeing a child drowning. Stepping into the water will ruin a pair of expensive shoes and soil one’s clothes, but walking past without acting constitutes a severe moral failure. The preservation of human life outweighs the preservation of personal footwear. Singer applies this physical scenario to foreign assistance. When an individual can prevent severe harm without sacrificing anything of comparable moral significance, an ethical obligation to act exists.<sup>2</sup>

Conventional ethics treats charitable donations as voluntary acts of personal generosity, worthy of praise when given and immune from criticism when withheld. Singer challenges that division. When a person has money to spare that could prevent starvation or fatal illness in a distant country, spending it on unnecessary consumer goods is a failure of basic moral duty.

Accepting Singer’s premise raises questions about the limits of moral duty. Must every spare penny go toward charity? Should someone choose a less fulfilling career because it would allow them to donate more? In *Moral Saints* (1982), Susan Wolf questions whether a life dedicated entirely to moral perfection would eliminate the pursuits that give human life its texture.<sup>3</sup> If every penny and waking hour must be directed toward alleviating suffering, personal ambitions and intimate relationships lose their independent value. The tension between Singer’s imperative to reduce suffering and Wolf’s defense of human flourishing defines the core dilemma of ethical giving.

#### When does generosity become a duty?

The drowning-child thought experiment derives its power from the imbalance between the stakes. On one side lies a human life; on the other lies the cost of wet clothing. Bystanders who refuse to wade into the water to rescue the child face condemnation because the required personal sacrifice is negligible compared to the catastrophe prevented.

Singer contends that physical separation does not alter the underlying moral relationship. A contribution to a relief agency provides medicine, clean drinking water, or medical treatment to someone in peril. Although the donor never meets the recipient and cannot watch the intervention unfold, the ethical components match the pond scenario: severe peril exists, and an individual possesses the capacity to intervene without endangering their own security.

Geography and national borders lack ethical relevance in evaluating human suffering. Geographical separation affects how strongly people feel about suffering, but the harm is equally severe whether it is nearby or thousands of miles away. Those equipped to relieve it therefore carry the same responsibility in both cases. A stranger collapsing nearby prompts an immediate impulse to intervene, whereas people suffering from famine thousands of miles away often appear as distant abstractions.

Accepting this obligation alters ordinary spending decisions. Purchasing luxury goods while people die of preventable diseases conflicts with Singer’s principle: the pleasure derived from possessions cannot outweigh the vital needs of people facing severe deprivation. When an individual can afford to give and has reliable channels to deliver aid, withholding assistance is an ethical failure.

Ethical requirements depend on context and feasibility. People cannot predict every outcome of their purchases, and relief organisations differ in their operational competence. Singer’s argument applies when the need is acute and the personal sacrifice remains proportionate.<sup>2</sup> Later debates about the demandingness of morality have examined whether such principles impose excessive requirements on ordinary people, particularly when sacrifices must be repeated indefinitely.<sup>4</sup> 

These conditions apply to many contemporary aid efforts. When assistance can prevent harm at manageable personal cost, withholding that help warrants moral criticism. Describing such aid as mere generosity misrepresents an ethical requirement as an optional luxury.

#### Must morality come before everything else?

Singer’s standard becomes increasingly demanding as one traces its logical conclusion. If every expenditure beyond basic sustenance should be converted into life-saving aid, then every hour of leisure and every professional decision must undergo strict utilitarian scrutiny. An individual would have to evaluate career options solely by their earning potential for philanthropic donation, turning daily life into an unrelenting calculation of utility.<sup>2,4</sup> 

Susan Wolf identifies the costs of such total devotion in *Moral Saints*. She analyses the concept of a person whose every action seeks the greatest moral good, whether out of pure altruism or strict obedience to duty. Wolf argues that such an ideal is fundamentally flawed because it excludes non-moral virtues. A moral saint cannot cultivate artistic talents or master crafts, because such endeavours consume resources that could relieve human hardship.

Human lives derive depth from commitments that resist moral calculation. Deep friendships demand loyalty and time that cannot be redistributed to unfamiliar strangers on utilitarian grounds. Creative endeavours and scientific inquiry hold value regardless of whether they produce quantifiable social benefits. Expecting individuals to evaluate every passion by its capacity to reduce global suffering ignores the distinct pursuits that give life purpose.

Wolf’s critique leaves the duty to relieve suffering in place. Giving up the ideal of the moral saint shrinks what is asked of individuals, and a duty to others remains. A person can decline to dedicate every waking hour to philanthropic work while still recognising that a portion of their resources should go toward relieving severe deprivation.

Singer demonstrates that comfortable individuals have duties toward those enduring extreme poverty, preventing people from treating benevolence as a mere whim.<sup>2</sup>  Wolf establishes that ethical obligations must not extinguish personal identity and cultural pursuits.<sup>3</sup>  A coherent framework expects substantial contributions from those who can comfortably afford them, while preserving their freedom to maintain personal projects and deep relationships.

#### How much do we owe?

Determining the boundaries of individual responsibility requires assessing both the recipient’s vulnerability and the donor’s situation. Foregoing a luxury holiday to finance medical treatments involves a different kind of sacrifice than skipping a meal to send funds abroad. A fair ethical standard accounts for the weight of what a person relinquishes, distinguishing between money they can give up and money they need for personal security.

Singer’s argument creates discomfort because it targets habitual spending that comfortable households take for granted. Upgrading a functional smartphone and dining at expensive restaurants are discretionary choices that could easily fund life-saving treatments. A substantial donation leaves an affluent family’s physical well-being and long-term financial security intact, and it brings essential relief to people facing destitution.

Immanuel Kant provides a framework that reconciles obligation with personal discretion. In Kant’s ethics, beneficence is an imperfect duty. Individuals must adopt the well-being of others as a practical goal, with latitude to decide on which occasions and in what amounts.<sup>5</sup>  This concept acknowledges that people possess legitimate personal ends and cannot assist every person in need, while insisting that they must actively integrate helping others into their life plans. Kant develops this account of duties towards others in The Metaphysics of Morals, particularly in the Doctrine of Virtue.<sup>5</sup>

Proportionality requires recognising disparities in financial capacity. A person working multiple low-wage jobs to pay for heating and rent cannot bear the same expectations as a corporate executive with substantial disposable wealth. Demanding monetary contributions from those who struggle to meet their own basic needs causes deprivation. Respect for human dignity means that those with extensive financial reserves carry a far greater duty to contribute than those facing economic instability.

Care obligations also affect an individual’s capacity to give. A person caring for an ageing parent or a chronically ill child devotes energy to fulfilling immediate moral duties. These personal responsibilities limit the time and financial resources available for philanthropic efforts. Moral duty operates within the context of existing commitments, requiring flexible judgment.

The traditions mentioned at the start of this essay answer the question of _how much_. _Zakat_, one of the five pillars of Islam, requires Muslims whose wealth exceeds a minimum threshold, known as the _nisab_, to pay about 2.5% of their savings each year, once those savings have been held for a full lunar year. The Qur’an names eight categories of recipient, including the poor and people in debt, and describes the poor as holding a rightful share in the wealth of the believers. Kant’s distinction fits this arrangement; _zakat_ is a perfect duty, with a fixed sum and specified recipients, and voluntary giving, known as _sadaqah_, sits above it with the amount left to the giver’s judgement.

The structure addresses several problems: in Singer’s formulation, the obligation continues until further giving would cost something of comparable importance, and Kant’s imperfect duty leaves each person to decide when they have done enough. A fixed rate gives both the generous and the comfortable a line. Those whose wealth falls below the threshold owe nothing, which builds the proportionality discussed above into the rule itself, and a person with greater savings pays a larger sum at the same rate. Because the floor is set, Wolf’s concern is also met: anything beyond it is left to personal discretion, so the demand never grows to cover a whole life.

#### Does helping have to mean donating?

Financial giving is only one avenue for fulfilling moral responsibilities. Providing hands-on care and volunteering specialised professional skills contribute directly to alleviating distress. Individuals who lack substantial financial savings often possess specialised skills or available time that can address urgent human needs with remarkable efficacy.

Recognising diverse forms of assistance ensures that ethical expectations reflect human differences. A physician can contribute specialised medical expertise, while a neighbour might provide dependable childcare to single parents. An ethical model centred solely on bank transfers overlooks the value of personal labour and relational care.

Philosophers associated with effective altruism, such as William MacAskill, highlight the necessity of evaluating the actual results of charitable action. Good intentions do not guarantee positive outcomes, and poorly designed interventions can waste resources or disrupt local economies. Evaluating empirical evidence helps donors direct their contributions toward initiatives that produce verified improvements in health and living standards.<sup>6</sup>

Yet quantifiable metrics cannot capture every dimension of human assistance. Some obligations arise from personal bonds, such as caring for a sick relative or supporting a struggling friend, where substituting a more cost-effective global intervention would betray the relationship itself. Special duties toward family members and immediate neighbours retain moral legitimacy alongside the broader obligation to relieve suffering across the world.

#### Who is responsible for helping?

Systemic problems like endemic poverty and inadequate healthcare infrastructure operate on a scale that individual philanthropy cannot resolve alone. Voluntary donations can supply emergency food or fund localised clinics, but private charity cannot guarantee comprehensive social protections across an entire population. Relying exclusively on private generosity leaves vulnerable populations exposed to donor preferences and economic cycles.

Pogge’s work reiterates this question of responsibility in World Poverty and Human Rights. He argues that severe poverty is sustained by the global economic order. Part of that order is a set of trade rules shaped largely by wealthy states. Another part is two privileges that international law grants to whoever holds effective power in a country: the resource privilege that lets that group sell the country’s natural resources, and the borrowing privilege that lets it take on debt in the country’s name. Both apply however the group came to power, including by force.<sup>7</sup> If affluent societies design these rules and benefit from them, they contribute to the harm that aid then tries to relieve. The duty at stake here is a negative one: a duty not to harm. It covers imposing or upholding unjust institutions. Like any duty not to harm, it is owed to the people affected as a matter of justice, whatever the giver chooses to do for them. Singer’s pond case describes a duty of beneficence to a stranger we did not endanger, while Pogge’s argument concerns people who are poor, on his account, in part because of the system the wealthy maintain. Critics dispute how much poverty global rules cause and point to domestic factors such as governance and corruption, so the empirical claim remains contested.<sup>8</sup>

Carnegie’s career shows the same structure on a smaller scale. He gave away most of his fortune, which was built at steelworks such as Homestead, where a wage dispute in 1892 led to a lockout and a gun battle that killed ten people.<sup>9</sup> On Pogge’s reasoning, the first question to ask about such a fortune concerns what its holder owes because of how it was made, before any question about how much he chooses to give. Charity can supply emergency food or fund clinics, as the previous paragraph notes, and an obligation grounded in justice calls for changing the arrangements that produce the need. That is why the state, with its power to alter rules and redistribute through taxation, belongs at the centre of the answer.

Governments possess unique capabilities and distinct moral mandates. Public institutions can raise revenue through progressive taxation and establish legal entitlements to basic necessities. State programmes distribute financial responsibility across society as a whole, preventing the burden of support from falling exclusively on exceptionally generous individuals.

Individual obligations remain in place alongside state responsibility. Public institutions often fail to reach marginalised groups or respond swiftly during acute emergencies. Individual contributions and civic engagement address immediate gaps in official services while pressuring governments to uphold their systemic duties. Personal giving and political advocacy reinforce one another in the pursuit of justice.

An ethical danger arises when private charity becomes a justification for state abdication. If communities accept private food banks and crowd-funded medical appeals as the standard response to human deprivation, pressure on public institutions to guarantee basic survival diminishes. Fulfilling personal duties of aid must occur without treating voluntary giving as an adequate substitute for reliable public systems.

#### What do we owe to others?

Classifying all assistance as voluntary generosity shields affluent individuals from ethical evaluation. Peter Singer demonstrates that when a person can prevent profound suffering at a proportionate personal cost, aiding others constitutes an obligation. Distance and unfamiliarity do not diminish this duty.<sup>2</sup>

Susan Wolf’s critique protects the diversity of human life from being suppressed under single-minded moral demands. An ethical life can include personal passions and loving relationships.<sup>3</sup> A coherent moral life balances responsiveness to urgent global needs with the pursuit of personal projects.

Clear numerical formulas cannot dictate the exact boundary between personal expenditure and obligatory giving for every individual. People face diverse financial circumstances and family commitments. Yet this complexity demands sustained personal reflection, not simple indifference.

Assisting people in severe peril should be expected as an ethical duty. When human need is acute and the required sacrifice remains proportionate, refusing to help warrants moral criticism. Distinguishing between obligation and discretionary generosity is an important step toward building an honest and responsible moral life.
