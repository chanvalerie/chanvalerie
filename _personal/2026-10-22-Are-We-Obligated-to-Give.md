# Are We Obligated to Give?

Imagine walking past a pond and seeing a child drowning in it. Pulling the child out would ruin your clothes and make you late, but the cost would be insignificant compared with the life you could save. Most people would consider it wrong to walk away. The inconvenience hardly seems to justify leaving the child to die.

In his 1972 essay *Famine, Affluence, and Morality*, philosopher Peter Singer uses a similar example to examine our obligations towards people living in extreme poverty. If someone can prevent suffering without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, Singer argues, they ought to do so. The fact that someone in need lives thousands of miles away does not make their suffering less deserving of help.

Singer's argument challenges the familiar view that charitable giving belongs to the realm of generosity. Donations and volunteering are usually praised as admirable acts, while declining to participate is rarely treated as a moral failure. His reasoning unsettles that division. If a person can save a life by giving up a luxury, what makes keeping the money morally acceptable?

Accepting that helping others can be obligatory, however, brings its own difficulties. Must every spare pound go towards charity? Should someone choose a less fulfilling career because it would allow them to donate more? In her essay *Moral Saints* (1982), Susan Wolf questions whether a life devoted entirely to moral goodness would even be desirable. Her argument offers a challenge to Singer's position: morality may demand too much if every personal decision becomes an opportunity to do more good.

The tension between these views matters for anyone trying to decide what they owe to others. Helping people should carry more moral significance than an optional act of kindness, particularly when suffering could be prevented at little cost. At the same time, an obligation to help need not require a life devoted exclusively to helping. The task is to establish what people owe to others, how much they should be expected to sacrifice, and where their moral responsibilities end.

## When does generosity become a duty?

The drowning-child example draws much of its force from the apparent simplicity of the choice. The child faces an immediate threat, the rescue is within reach, and the cost to the bystander is minor. Walking away would mean allowing a preventable death for the sake of dry clothes. Few people would defend that decision.

Singer asks why the judgement should change when the person in danger lives far away. A donation to an effective aid organisation might help provide food, clean water or medical treatment to someone facing conditions that threaten their life. The donor never encounters the recipient, and the consequences of giving may be less visible than a rescue from a pond. The underlying moral considerations remain similar: someone needs help, another person can provide it, and the sacrifice required may be modest.

Distance, familiarity and visibility influence how people respond to suffering. A person struggling in front of us can demand immediate attention, while people experiencing poverty elsewhere remain abstract. Donations can be postponed, forgotten or dismissed as someone else's responsibility. Singer's argument challenges the moral relevance of these differences. If a person's need is equally urgent, geographical distance alone offers little reason to care less.

The implications extend beyond occasional donations. If helping is obligatory whenever the cost is proportionate to the good achieved, charitable giving cannot be left entirely to personal preference. A person who spends money on an unnecessary luxury while knowing that the same amount could prevent substantial suffering has a moral choice to account for. Enjoyment and ownership do not automatically outweigh another person's need.

This does not mean every uncharitable purchase deserves condemnation. People cannot know the consequences of every possible use of their money, and not every organisation produces the same results. Nor does every request for assistance create an equal claim on a person's resources. Singer's argument is strongest when the need is substantial, the opportunity to help is credible and the sacrifice required is comparatively small.

Those conditions describe a meaningful portion of charitable giving. They also expose a weakness in the idea that generosity deserves admiration while withholding help requires no explanation. When assistance could prevent serious harm at a modest personal cost, refusing to provide it may be morally wrong. Calling the alternative generosity makes the act sound optional when the circumstances suggest otherwise.

## Must morality come before everything else?

Singer's argument becomes more demanding as the cost of helping rises. If a donation can save a life, keeping the money for personal enjoyment becomes harder to justify. If the same reasoning applies to every spare hour, every career decision and every non-essential expense, a person's life could become an ongoing calculation of how much more good they might accomplish.

Susan Wolf's *Moral Saints* examines the appeal and limitations of this ideal. Wolf considers people whose lives are devoted to moral goodness, whether through constant benevolence towards others or through the pursuit of moral perfection. Such people might be generous, conscientious and deeply committed to helping those in need. Wolf nevertheless questions whether they would make desirable models for everyone else.

A life organised entirely around moral service can leave little room for pursuits that have value without producing a clear benefit to others. Music, sport, humour, artistic ambition and the pleasure of spending an afternoon with friends might all seem secondary when measured against unmet human needs. A person who continually asks how each activity could be redirected towards helping others risks treating personal enjoyment as something that must always justify itself.

Wolf's concern is persuasive because human lives contain commitments that cannot be reduced to charitable impact. Friendships require time. Family relationships involve obligations that may not produce measurable outcomes. Creative work and intellectual curiosity can be worth pursuing for their own sake. A person should not have to prove that every interest contributes to the welfare of strangers before being allowed to enjoy it.

The objection becomes less convincing, however, if it is used to excuse any refusal to help. Wolf challenges the ideal of moral sainthood; she does not establish that people have no duties towards others. A person can reject the expectation of devoting every waking hour to charity while still accepting that some portion of their resources should be used to prevent suffering. The choice need not lie between total self-sacrifice and complete indifference.

This is where Singer and Wolf can be read as addressing different aspects of moral life. Singer draws attention to the suffering that people could prevent, while Wolf asks what happens when the demand to prevent it becomes the organising principle of a person's entire existence. The first argument prevents personal freedom from becoming an easy excuse for neglect. The second prevents moral responsibility from swallowing every other source of value.

An obligation to help should therefore leave room for a life beyond helping. Personal interests and relationships have legitimate claims on time and resources, although they cannot automatically override the needs of others. The existence of a duty does not make every competing desire equally important.

## How much do we owe?

Accepting a duty to help still leaves the question of how much someone should give. A donation that requires skipping an expensive meal differs from one that leaves a person unable to pay their bills. Volunteering for an afternoon differs from abandoning responsibilities to a child or dependent relative. The moral cost of assistance depends on the circumstances of the person providing it as well as the needs of the recipient.

Singer's reasoning places pressure on comfortable lifestyles. If people can afford repeated luxuries while others lack the means to meet basic needs, some of that spending deserves moral scrutiny. The argument becomes uncomfortable precisely because the money available for giving often comes from choices that feel ordinary: replacing a working phone, taking an expensive holiday or buying something simply because it is desirable. A donation need not threaten a person's standard of living to make a meaningful difference to someone else.

A strict application of Singer's principle could require substantial sacrifices well beyond what most people currently regard as charitable. Wolf's objection helps explain why that expectation may be excessive, but it cannot determine the appropriate limit on its own. The fact that a morally perfect life would be undesirable does not tell us whether someone should donate five per cent of their income, ten per cent, or another amount. Any fixed figure would need further justification.

Immanuel Kant offers a different account of beneficence. In his moral philosophy, helping others is an imperfect duty: people must adopt the welfare of others as an end, but retain discretion over how they fulfil that duty. They are not required to help every person in need or to respond to every opportunity in the same way. This flexibility recognises that moral responsibility operates alongside other duties and commitments.

Kant's account offers a useful basis for understanding an obligation that does not demand total self-sacrifice. People should take the needs of others seriously and make room for helping them, while retaining some freedom to decide how to contribute. The duty cannot be fulfilled merely by feeling sympathy; it must influence action. However, it need not dictate every decision.

A proportionate obligation also has to account for what people can reasonably afford. Someone living on a low income may already struggle to meet their own basic needs. Expecting them to donate money that would otherwise pay for food or heating would ignore the cost of the sacrifice. Someone with considerable disposable income faces a different situation. Equal moral concern for those in need does not require identical donations from people with unequal means.

The same principle applies to time. A person with substantial caring responsibilities may have limited capacity to volunteer, while someone with more freedom might be able to contribute regularly. Neither money nor time should be treated as the only measure of moral commitment. What matters is whether a person takes reasonable opportunities to help, given the demands already placed on them.

The precise boundary will remain open to disagreement. Still, uncertainty about the exact amount owed does not make the obligation meaningless. People regularly make judgements about competing responsibilities without possessing a formula that resolves every case. The absence of a universal donation threshold is no reason to treat all charitable giving as optional.

## Does helping have to mean donating?

Money offers one direct way to help, but an obligation to assist others need not prescribe a single method. Volunteering, caring for someone in need, offering professional skills and supporting useful community work can all contribute to other people's welfare. A person who cannot afford a substantial donation may have time or knowledge that can be put to good use.

The freedom to choose how to help also matters because people have different abilities and circumstances. A medical professional may be able to contribute through their work; someone with financial security may be able to make regular donations; another person may provide essential care within their family. A moral framework that recognises only financial giving risks overlooking forms of assistance that are valuable but less visible.

Choice does not remove the need to consider whether help actually helps. A well-intentioned donation can be ineffective, and volunteering may consume resources without meeting the needs of the people it aims to serve. Effective altruism, associated with philosophers such as William MacAskill, encourages people to compare ways of doing good and consider where their contributions can achieve the most benefit. Its emphasis on evidence offers a useful corrective to the assumption that good intentions are sufficient.

However, measuring impact cannot resolve every question about how people should live. Some forms of care involve personal relationships that cannot be replaced simply because another intervention might produce a greater measurable benefit. A person may reasonably prioritise a dependent relative over an unfamiliar recipient, even when another use of their time or money could produce a larger aggregate benefit. Duties arising from relationships can carry moral importance alongside the duty to help strangers.

The obligation to help therefore leaves room for judgement about both the method and the recipient. That freedom should not become an excuse to choose only convenient forms of assistance or to ignore evidence that a contribution is ineffective. It allows people to fulfil their responsibilities in ways that fit their circumstances while still taking the needs of others seriously.

## Who is responsible for helping?

An obligation to help can also be understood as a shared responsibility. Poverty, illness and social exclusion affect people on a scale that individual donations cannot fully address. Charities can provide essential services, but they cannot independently guarantee access to healthcare, housing, education or income security for everyone who needs them.

Public institutions have responsibilities that individual donors do not. Governments can raise revenue, establish social protections and coordinate services across an entire population. These arrangements can make assistance more consistent than a system that depends on whether individuals happen to donate. They can also distribute the costs of meeting social needs across a wider group rather than leaving those costs to people who happen to be particularly generous.

Recognising institutional responsibility does not remove individual obligations. Public services cannot anticipate every need, and people may still encounter suffering that they can address directly. Donations and volunteering can support services, respond to urgent situations and meet needs that formal systems overlook. Individual action and collective provision can complement one another.

A danger arises when charitable giving becomes a substitute for asking whether essential needs should depend on private goodwill at all. If access to food, shelter or medical treatment rests entirely on voluntary contributions, those who receive help remain vulnerable to the priorities and resources of donors. A person can fulfil a personal duty to help without assuming that private charity is an adequate replacement for public provision.

This also clarifies why an obligation to help cannot mean that every individual must personally solve every problem they encounter. People can contribute to collective solutions through political participation, support for public services and their choices as members of a community. Their responsibility extends beyond writing cheques, although the form it takes will depend on their circumstances and opportunities.

The presence of wider institutional duties should not make personal action seem insignificant. Someone who can prevent suffering at a proportionate cost still has a reason to act, even when governments and organisations also bear responsibility. The existence of a public safety net does not make a preventable injury morally irrelevant; nor does the existence of charity excuse institutions from addressing the conditions that make assistance necessary.

## What do we owe to others?

Treating charitable giving as a purely voluntary act makes it easy to praise generosity without examining the choices that precede it. Singer's argument exposes the weakness of that approach. When people can prevent substantial suffering at a modest cost, helping can be a duty rather than an exceptional act of kindness. The fact that assistance takes place across national borders, or that the recipient remains unknown to the donor, does not erase that responsibility.

Wolf's challenge nevertheless deserves to remain part of the argument. A life devoted exclusively to maximising moral good would leave insufficient room for the relationships, interests and ambitions that give human lives their character. People should not be expected to treat every hour of leisure as a missed opportunity to help someone else. Moral responsibility must coexist with other legitimate purposes.

The boundary between obligation and generosity will never be perfectly precise. People differ in their resources, commitments and capacity to help, and some circumstances demand more than others. That uncertainty should encourage careful judgement rather than allow people to dismiss the duty altogether.

Helping others should be expected, not merely celebrated. The obligation is strongest when the need is substantial, the opportunity to help is credible and the personal cost is proportionate. It leaves room for people to pursue their own lives, but asks them to recognise that personal freedom exists alongside responsibilities to those whose suffering they can prevent. Generosity may deserve praise; refusing to help when the sacrifice is modest may deserve criticism. Acknowledging that difference is the first step towards taking moral responsibility seriously.
