# Are We Obligated to Give?

In 1889, Andrew Carnegie published an essay arguing that a man who dies rich dies disgraced. He believed that great fortunes should be used for the public good, and he subsequently funded public libraries and educational institutions across the United States and beyond.<sup>1</sup> Three years later, workers at his Homestead steelworks in Pennsylvania went on strike in response to a dispute over wages and working conditions. Carnegie’s business partner, Henry Clay Frick, brought in Pinkerton guards to secure the plant, leading to a violent confrontation in which several workers and guards were killed. Carnegie was in Scotland at the time, but he remained in communication with Frick and supported his handling of the dispute.<sup>2</sup>

Carnegie’s philanthropy and his industrial career bring a central problem into focus: what makes giving morally good? His donations expanded access to education, yet the fortune behind them was accumulated through a business in which workers fought for better pay and conditions, sometimes at the cost of their lives. Can the good achieved through philanthropy make up for the harm associated with how wealth was acquired, or must a person’s generosity be judged alongside their responsibilities to others?

The question extends beyond wealthy industrialists. Giving is often treated as a personal choice, an expression of generosity that deserves praise but cannot be demanded. If a person can improve someone else’s life without making a substantial sacrifice, however, keeping their resources for themselves becomes harder to defend. The moral status of giving depends on what people owe one another, how much they can be expected to sacrifice, and whether private generosity can address needs that arise from wider social arrangements.

In his 1972 essay *Famine, Affluence, and Morality*, the philosopher Peter Singer explores this problem through a simple hypothetical. A person walking to work passes a child drowning in a shallow pond. The child can be rescued, though doing so will ruin the person's clothes and make them late. Most people would consider it wrong to walk past because the cost of helping seems negligible compared with the child's life. Singer argues that the same reasoning should apply to people suffering from extreme poverty, even when they live far away. If someone can prevent serious suffering without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, they ought to do so. Geographic distance, in itself, does not justify withholding help.<sup>3</sup>

The argument leads to four questions:

A) Are people morally obligated to give, or should helping others remain a voluntary act of generosity?

B) Does possessing greater wealth create a greater obligation to help?

C) Must giving take the form of money?

D) Can individual charity compensate for the social conditions that produce poverty in the first place?

These questions concern different aspects of the same moral problem. Singer focuses on the consequences of withholding help, while Immanuel Kant treats beneficence as a duty that allows individuals discretion over how they fulfil it. Arguments about wealth introduce questions of ownership and justice, while debates about philanthropy examine the power that donors acquire when they decide which public needs deserve attention. The answers depend on how these obligations are understood and on whether they arise from individual circumstances, existing inequalities or the institutions through which wealth is distributed.

#### A) Are people morally obligated to give, or should helping others remain a voluntary act of generosity?

Singer’s argument challenges the familiar separation between charity and duty. Donations are commonly understood as admirable acts that go beyond what morality requires. Failing to donate may be regrettable, but it rarely attracts the same condemnation as directly harming another person. Singer disputes this arrangement. If preventing suffering is within someone’s power and requires no sacrifice of comparable importance, the fact that others also have an opportunity to help does not remove their own obligation.<sup>3</sup>

The implications extend well beyond the drowning child. A person who spends money on luxuries while knowing that the same money could fund food or medical treatment faces a choice between personal enjoyment and preventing suffering. Singer’s argument does not make that choice morally neutral simply because the money already belongs to the person spending it. Ownership establishes control over a resource, but it does not automatically establish that every use of it is justified.

His position becomes demanding when applied consistently. If an individual must give whenever doing so prevents greater suffering, the amount they can keep for themselves becomes difficult to define. Singer argues that people should continue giving until further sacrifice would involve losing something of comparable moral importance. Ordinary comforts and discretionary spending may therefore carry obligations that many people would ordinarily associate with exceptional generosity.

Critics have questioned whether morality can place such extensive demands on personal life. Susan Wolf’s essay “Moral Saints” examines whether a person who devotes nearly every decision to moral improvement would have room for the interests, relationships and pursuits that make a life their own.<sup>4</sup> Bernard Williams similarly argues that an ethical theory can become alienating when it requires individuals to view their deepest commitments primarily through the demands of impartial morality.<sup>5</sup> Someone who spends time supporting a friend, pursuing music or caring for family may have reasons to value those activities beyond the amount of benefit they produce for others.

These objections do not establish that helping others is optional. They challenge the extent to which moral obligations should govern personal choices. Singer’s account gives priority to preventing suffering; his critics question whether this priority leaves sufficient room for commitments that cannot be measured by their contribution to overall welfare.

Kant offers another way to understand the obligation. In *The Metaphysics of Morals*, he treats beneficence as an imperfect duty: people must adopt the happiness and wellbeing of others as ends, but they retain discretion over how they fulfil that duty.<sup>6</sup> The duty is binding even though it does not prescribe a fixed amount to donate or require every available resource to be directed towards others. A person must make helping others part of their conduct, while exercising judgement about which needs to address and how.

This account allows more room for personal commitments than Singer’s principle, although it does not reduce beneficence to a matter of preference. A person cannot claim to fulfil their obligations simply by admiring generosity in the abstract. Their choices must include some practical concern for the wellbeing of others.

The disagreement concerns what morality can demand from an individual. Singer offers a principle that places substantial limits on spending resources for personal benefit when those resources could prevent greater suffering. Kant recognises a duty to help while leaving its fulfilment open to judgement. The distinction affects how people should assess their own conduct, as well as how they judge others who give different amounts.

#### B) Does possessing greater wealth create a greater obligation to help?

Wealth changes what a person can do for others and what they must give up to do it. A donation that requires one person to forgo an essential expense may require another to surrender a small portion of discretionary income. Singer’s argument implies that the second person can often give more without sacrificing something of comparable importance. Their greater capacity creates a stronger obligation to help, even if the same nominal donation would count as generous from someone with fewer resources.<sup>3</sup>

Carnegie’s *Wealth* offers a related argument from the perspective of the wealthy. He maintained that people who accumulate great fortunes should administer their surplus for public benefit during their lifetimes or arrange for its use after death.<sup>1</sup> His position assigned wealthy individuals a responsibility that extended beyond personal consumption. It also left them considerable authority to decide which causes deserved support and how their money should be spent.

The moral basis of this responsibility remains contested. One argument begins with capacity: wealthy people can often give substantial sums while preserving a comfortable standard of living. Another concerns the origins of their wealth. If an individual's fortune depends on arrangements that distribute resources unfairly, the obligation to give may arise from what they owe others rather than from their ability to spare money.

Chiara Cordelli’s work on philanthropy challenges the assumption that wealthy people are always entitled to treat their surplus as private property available for discretionary use. If institutions distribute wealth unjustly, the resulting holdings may exceed what their owners are morally entitled to keep. Giving can therefore involve a responsibility to repair injustice, rather than an optional decision to share something that belongs entirely to the donor.<sup>7</sup> Cordelli develops related arguments in *The Privatized State*, examining the relationship between private philanthropy and public institutions.<sup>8</sup>

Robert Nozick’s account of entitlement offers a contrasting view. In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, he argues that holdings are just when they arise through legitimate acquisition and transfer, subject to the qualifications in his theory of justice.<sup>9</sup> If a person is entitled to their wealth, the fact that someone else needs it does not, by itself, establish a right to receive it. This approach gives property rights a central role in determining the limits of compulsory redistribution.

The disagreement cannot be resolved simply by observing that wealthy people have more money. Capacity to help, entitlement to property and responsibility for unjust arrangements are separate grounds for obligation. They may lead to overlapping conclusions in some cases, but they do not offer the same explanation for why a person should give.

The Giving Pledge, launched by Bill Gates and Warren Buffett in 2010, illustrates how voluntary philanthropy operates among the very wealthy. Signatories publicly commit to giving away the majority of their wealth, either during their lifetimes or through their wills.<sup>10</sup> The pledge does not legally compel contributions or prescribe a common distribution of funds. Donors retain considerable discretion over recipients and causes.

Rob Reich argues that large-scale philanthropy also creates questions about democratic equality. Wealthy donors can influence education, research and public policy through decisions that are not subject to the same accountability as public spending.<sup>11</sup> A donation may benefit its recipients while giving its donor considerable influence over which problems receive attention. The capacity to give therefore carries questions about the power to decide what other people need.

Whether wealth creates a greater duty to give depends on the basis of the duty itself. A person may owe more because they can help at a lower personal cost, because their wealth exceeds what justice permits them to retain, or because their financial power allows them to shape institutions that affect others. These arguments overlap, but each places a different limit on the freedom to keep and spend private wealth.

#### C) Must giving take the form of money?

Money offers a convenient way to measure giving. Donations can be recorded, compared and directed towards needs identified by recipients or organisations. Financial contributions also allow people to support work beyond their immediate surroundings, including medical treatment, disaster relief and research into neglected diseases. Their value depends on how effectively the funds address the needs for which they are intended.

Giving can also involve time and practical assistance. Someone may volunteer at a food bank, help a neighbour navigate a language barrier or care for a person who cannot manage alone. These acts do not always involve a financial transfer, and their value may depend on the relationship between the people involved. A person who needs companionship or direct assistance may benefit from another's time in ways that money cannot fully replace.

Aristotle’s account of generosity in the *Nicomachean Ethics* places emphasis on the judgement involved in giving, including how much a person gives and whether the gift is appropriate to their circumstances.<sup>12</sup> Generosity is not simply the transfer of resources. It involves the way a person understands their means and uses them in relation to others.

Time, however, is not automatically more valuable than money. Effective altruism examines how resources can be used to produce the greatest benefit, including the possibility of earning more in order to donate more. In *Doing Good Better*, William MacAskill discusses earning to give as one route through which people can contribute to effective causes.<sup>13</sup> Someone with a high earning potential may be able to fund work that achieves more than their direct volunteering could, depending on the job, the donation and the alternative uses of their time.

The argument does not make salary the sole measure of a person's contribution. Work can have harmful consequences, and donations do not automatically compensate for them. The source of money and the effects of its use remain relevant. A high income may increase the capacity to help while also creating questions about how that income was generated.

Giving goods rather than money introduces another set of considerations. After a disaster, donors may send clothing, food or household items that are not needed by the people receiving them. Unrequested donations can occupy storage space and require transport, sorting and disposal. Relief organisations often ask for financial contributions because these allow them to purchase supplies suited to local needs.

This does not mean that in-kind giving is always inappropriate. In some circumstances, goods are urgently needed and money cannot secure them quickly enough. The decision depends on the conditions faced by recipients and on the resources available to deliver assistance.

Work by political philosopher Megan A. K. Saunders-Hastings examines the relationship between benevolent giving and paternalism: the risk that donors impose their own judgement about what others need instead of respecting recipients’ agency.<sup>14</sup> A donation can be well-intentioned while placing the donor’s preferences above the choices of the people it is supposed to help. Giving becomes more responsive when recipients can influence what they receive and how assistance is delivered.

The form of a gift cannot determine its moral value on its own. Money, time and goods serve different purposes, and each can be used well or badly. The relevant considerations include what recipients need, what the giver can offer and whether the contribution improves the recipient’s circumstances.

#### D) Can individual charity compensate for the social conditions that produce poverty in the first place?

A food bank can help someone who cannot afford groceries that week. Its work may prevent hunger, but it does not necessarily change the rent, wages or public benefits that determine whether the same person can afford food next month. The immediate benefit of charity can be substantial even when the conditions that created the need remain in place.

This is one of the central criticisms of private philanthropy. Will Kymlicka’s introduction to contemporary political philosophy examines how theories of justice assess the distribution of resources and the institutions that shape people’s opportunities.<sup>15</sup> From this perspective, poverty cannot be understood solely as a collection of individual hardships requiring charitable responses. The rules governing employment, housing, taxation and public services help determine who has access to resources in the first place.

Charity can relieve the consequences of an unjust arrangement while leaving that arrangement intact. If a donor funds a service that would otherwise be publicly provided, the donation may benefit those who use it, but it can also leave decisions about access and priorities in private hands. Whether this is desirable depends on the service, the alternatives available and the accountability of those making decisions.

John Stuart Mill’s *Principles of Political Economy* examines the relationship between economic institutions and the distribution of wealth, including the extent to which poverty reflects social arrangements rather than unavoidable scarcity.<sup>16</sup> Such arguments shift attention from the generosity of individual donors to the institutions that shape material conditions. Helping someone meet an immediate need remains valuable, but it cannot by itself establish that the wider distribution of resources is just.

The distinction between charity and justice also affects what can be demanded of individuals. Charity is commonly associated with voluntary action, while justice concerns what people are entitled to claim and what institutions are required to secure. If access to food, shelter or medical care is understood as a matter of justice, meeting those needs cannot depend entirely on whether a donor chooses to help.

Allen Buchanan’s work on justice and charity considers the circumstances under which duties to aid others may become enforceable obligations, rather than remaining matters of personal discretion.<sup>17</sup> Institutional arrangements can assign responsibilities, collect resources and establish claims that individuals can make against one another through public rules. Taxation and social provision offer ways to distribute the costs of meeting needs across a population, rather than relying exclusively on voluntary contributions.

Religious traditions also provide accounts of giving that connect personal conduct with collective responsibility. In Islam, zakat is an obligatory form of almsgiving for Muslims who meet the relevant conditions. Its rules specify eligible recipients and the basis on which contributions are calculated. In Indonesia, the National Board of Zakat, or BAZNAS, coordinates aspects of zakat collection and distribution.<sup>18</sup> Zakat is not identical to taxation, but it illustrates a tradition in which giving is understood as an obligation rather than a discretionary act of generosity.

Cordelli’s account of philanthropy brings these questions back to the ownership of wealth. If a person's holdings exceed what they are entitled to under just institutions, giving alone may be insufficient. Their responsibilities could also extend to supporting changes in the arrangements through which wealth and opportunities are distributed.<sup>7, 8</sup> The obligation would not be exhausted by choosing a worthy recipient; it would include attention to the conditions that make the transfer necessary.

Private philanthropy can still contribute to work that governments neglect or struggle to support. Donors may fund experimental research, respond quickly to emerging needs or support community organisations whose work does not fit established public priorities. These contributions can achieve valuable results. Their value does not remove the need to examine who sets priorities, who can access the benefits and whether public institutions are fulfilling their own responsibilities.

Charity and justice therefore address overlapping concerns through different means. Voluntary giving can respond to immediate need and support work that would otherwise go unfunded. Questions of justice concern the social arrangements that distribute resources and determine who is responsible for ensuring that people can meet their needs. A society may need both, while still requiring an account of what each can and cannot achieve.

#### What follows from the duty to give?

The arguments examined here offer different explanations for why people should help others. Singer grounds the obligation in the prevention of suffering; Kant treats beneficence as a duty while allowing discretion in its fulfilment. Cordelli challenges the assumption that wealth is always available for unrestricted private use, while Nozick gives legitimate property holdings a central role in defining the limits of what can be demanded. Their conclusions cannot be combined into a single rule without overlooking disagreements about ownership, personal freedom and justice.

The amount a person ought to give remains contested. Singer’s principle can require substantial sacrifice when further giving would prevent greater suffering. Kant’s account leaves room for judgement about how beneficence should be practised. Carnegie’s ideal assigns wealthy people a responsibility to use their surplus for public benefit, while zakat specifies obligations within a religious framework. The Giving Pledge expresses a voluntary commitment to donate the majority of one’s wealth. Each approach draws its obligations from different moral or social commitments.

Money is one way of helping, but it does not capture every form of contribution. Time and practical assistance can meet needs that financial support cannot address on its own. At the same time, the personal meaning of a gift should not obscure its consequences for the recipient. A contribution should be considered in relation to the need it addresses and the alternatives available.

Carnegie’s libraries remain a significant part of his legacy, extending access to education across many communities. The Homestead strike remains part of that history too, exposing the conflict between his public commitments and the actions of the company he controlled. Acknowledging the benefits of his philanthropy does not remove the question of his responsibilities to workers. Nor does the history of the strike mean that the libraries had no value.

The question of giving ultimately concerns more than the willingness to share what one possesses. It concerns what people are entitled to keep, what they owe to others and how those obligations should be fulfilled. Carnegie’s example brings these issues together in the life of one industrialist, but the same questions apply whenever private resources are used to address needs that extend beyond the person who owns them.
