#  Are We Obligated to Give?

In 1889, Andrew Carnegie published an essay arguing that a man who dies rich dies disgraced. He believed that great fortunes should be used for the public good, and he subsequently funded public libraries and educational institutions across the United States and beyond (Carnegie 1889). Three years later, workers at his Homestead steelworks in Pennsylvania went on strike in response to a dispute over wages and working conditions. Carnegie’s business partner, Henry Clay Frick, brought in Pinkerton guards to secure the plant, leading to a violent confrontation in which several workers and guards were killed. Carnegie was in Scotland at the time, but the events raised questions about the responsibilities of an industrialist whose fortune depended on the labour of others (Krause 1992).

Carnegie’s philanthropy and his industrial career raise a question about what makes giving morally good. His donations expanded access to education, yet the fortune behind them was accumulated through a business in which workers fought for better pay and conditions, sometimes at the cost of their lives. Can the good achieved through philanthropy make up for the harm associated with how wealth was acquired, or must a person’s generosity be judged alongside their responsibilities to others?

The question extends beyond wealthy industrialists. Giving is usually presented as a voluntary act, something that deserves praise without being demanded. Yet when people have the means to relieve suffering, it becomes harder to explain why helping should remain entirely a matter of personal preference.

This essay examines four questions:

A) Are people morally obligated to give, or should giving remain voluntary?
B) Does having greater wealth create a greater obligation to give?
C) Must giving take the form of money?
D) Can charity address poverty and injustice, or does it leave the causes of deprivation intact?

The questions overlap, but the answers depend on competing ideas about ownership, responsibility and the relationship between individuals and society. Peter Singer argues that people have demanding obligations to prevent suffering. Immanuel Kant treats beneficence as a moral duty while preserving discretion over its fulfilment. Other philosophers question whether private generosity can discharge obligations that properly belong to justice. Their disagreements shape how the actions of someone like Carnegie should be understood.

### A) Are people morally obligated to give, or should giving remain voluntary?

In his 1972 essay Famine, Affluence, and Morality, Peter Singer asks readers to imagine passing a child drowning in a shallow pond. Pulling the child out would ruin one's clothes and cause lateness for work. Most people would consider walking past morally unacceptable. The sacrifice involved in helping is minor compared with the life that could be saved.

Singer argues that distance does not change the underlying obligation. A person who can prevent severe suffering abroad at relatively little cost to themselves has a moral reason to do so, just as the passer-by has a reason to rescue the child. If the suffering can be prevented without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, Singer argues, the person ought to act (Singer 1972).

The argument challenges the boundary commonly drawn between what morality requires and what deserves praise. Donations are often treated as optional contributions beyond one's ordinary responsibilities. Under Singer's account, some giving belongs within the responsibilities themselves. Spending money on luxuries while knowing that a smaller amount could prevent serious harm becomes a moral choice that requires justification.

The implications reach beyond a single donation. If the obligation continues whenever money could be used to prevent greater suffering, a person may have to keep giving until further contributions would seriously compromise their own well-being or other important commitments. Singer's position places substantial demands on ordinary consumption, leaving less room for the assumption that income remaining after essential expenses is entirely one's own to spend.

Critics have questioned whether morality can reasonably make such extensive demands. Susan Wolf's essay Moral Saints (1982) examines what happens when the pursuit of moral goodness becomes the organising purpose of a person's life. Someone who directs nearly every decision towards helping others may have little space for interests that do not directly benefit anyone else. Bernard Williams raises a related concern about moral theories that require people to treat their own commitments as expendable whenever a greater benefit can be achieved elsewhere (Smart and Williams 1973).

These objections do not require indifference towards distant suffering. They question whether the duty to help should override nearly every other consideration. Relationships, personal projects and the freedom to decide how to live have moral significance of their own. A person may reasonably believe that they should donate regularly without accepting that every discretionary purchase must be judged against the lives it could save.

Kant offers a different account. In The Metaphysics of Morals, he identifies beneficence as an imperfect duty: people must adopt the happiness and well-being of others as ends, but they retain latitude over how to fulfil that duty (Kant 1797). The obligation to help is genuine, although it does not prescribe a fixed amount or require every available resource to be directed towards others.

The contrast with Singer concerns the extent of the obligation. Singer asks how much suffering a person can prevent before the sacrifice becomes morally comparable. Kant allows greater discretion in the way a person organises their moral life. Both accounts reject the idea that other people's needs can always be dismissed as irrelevant to private choices.

Whether giving is obligatory, then, depends partly on what counts as a moral requirement. Singer's account makes the consequences of withholding help central. Kant's account places beneficence among the duties that shape a person's conduct without specifying a single pattern of giving. The difference affects how we judge someone who donates a small amount consistently, someone who gives a large sum once, or someone who chooses to help through their time.

### B) Does having greater wealth create a greater obligation to give?

A donation of £1,000 can represent a serious sacrifice for someone on a low income and a negligible expense for someone with a fortune. The amount transferred is identical, but the circumstances of the donors are not. Any account of charitable obligation that ignores a person's means risks treating unequal sacrifices as though they were equivalent.

Singer's argument gives wealth a direct moral significance. The more resources a person has beyond what they need, the more they can give without sacrificing anything of comparable importance. A wealthy person may be able to prevent considerable suffering while making little change to their own standard of living. Someone living close to the poverty line may have much less capacity to do so.

The argument also challenges the idea that wealth automatically belongs to its owner in a morally unrestricted sense. Carnegie believed that great fortunes carried responsibilities towards the communities that made their accumulation possible. In The Gospel of Wealth, he argued that surplus wealth should be administered for the public good rather than left to accumulate or be passed down without purpose (Carnegie 1889).

Carnegie's position gave wealthy people considerable responsibility, but also considerable discretion. They could decide which institutions to fund and which projects deserved support. A philanthropist might finance a library, establish a university or fund scientific research. These decisions can produce substantial benefits, yet they allow one person to influence public priorities according to their own judgement.

The question of what wealthy people owe becomes more contentious when the source of their fortune is considered. Chiara Cordelli argues that philanthropy should be understood in relation to the justice of the institutions through which wealth is acquired. If unjust social arrangements allow some people to retain resources beyond what they would be entitled to under just conditions, their surplus may be owed to others rather than available for discretionary charity (Cordelli 2016, 2020).

This account changes the status of a donation. If a portion of someone's fortune is owed as a matter of justice, giving it away does not necessarily represent generosity. The act may amount to returning resources that should not have remained under their control. Carnegie's libraries would still have benefited their users, but their value to those communities would not by itself answer questions about how the fortune behind them was accumulated.

Robert Nozick's account of property offers a direct challenge to this reasoning. In Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), he argues that holdings acquired and transferred justly belong to their owners. On this view, a person's wealth does not automatically become available for redistribution simply because others could benefit from it. Voluntary giving can be admirable, but the existence of need does not, by itself, establish a right to someone else's property.

The disagreement depends on how ownership is understood. If property rights are considered legitimate regardless of the inequalities they produce, the case for obligatory redistribution becomes narrower. If ownership depends on the justice of the institutions that establish and protect it, then the moral standing of a fortune may depend on how those institutions operate.

Wealth also gives donors influence beyond the immediate value of their gifts. Rob Reich argues that large-scale philanthropy can create problems for democratic equality because wealthy individuals can shape public priorities through private decisions that are not subject to the same accountability as government spending (Reich 2018). A billionaire can direct substantial resources towards a preferred cause, while people affected by that decision may have little say in how the money is used.

The Giving Pledge, launched by Bill Gates and Warren Buffett in 2010, asks billionaires to commit to giving away the majority of their wealth during their lifetimes or through their wills. The pledge has encouraged public commitments to philanthropy, but it is voluntary and legally non-binding. Its participants retain control over which causes they support and when they give.

A commitment to give away most of a fortune may represent a substantial contribution to society. It also leaves open the question of whether public priorities should depend so heavily on the preferences of wealthy individuals. A donation can fund valuable work while concentrating decision-making power in private hands.

The wealthy may therefore have greater obligations because they can help at relatively little personal cost, because some holdings may derive from unjust arrangements, or because their control of resources gives them unusual power over others. These are separate arguments, and accepting one does not require accepting all the others.

### C) Must giving take the form of money?

Money is the most familiar measure of charitable giving, partly because it can be counted and compared. Donations can be reported in annual accounts, divided between causes and evaluated against a financial target. The same measures are less suited to forms of assistance that involve time, labour or care.

Someone who regularly supports an elderly relative may spend hours on tasks that would otherwise require paid assistance. A volunteer may help a community organisation continue operating when its budget cannot cover all its work. Neither contribution necessarily involves a financial transfer, yet both can relieve burdens that would otherwise fall on someone else.

Kant's account of beneficence allows for different forms of assistance because the duty concerns helping others, rather than making a donation of a prescribed size. Aristotle's discussion of generosity in the Nicomachean Ethics also emphasises judgement about how a person gives, including the circumstances and needs involved. Giving well requires more than parting with a resource.

Time, however, cannot always replace money. A person volunteering at a food bank may help distribute supplies, but the food must still be purchased or donated. Someone may be willing to provide care but lack the training needed to do so safely. The value of a contribution depends on what the recipient needs and whether the form of assistance can meet that need.

The effective altruism movement has raised this issue through the idea of earning to give. Rather than volunteering directly, someone with high earning potential may choose a well-paid career and donate a substantial share of their income to effective charities. William MacAskill argues that people should consider how much good their choices can achieve, including the opportunity cost of the time they spend on one activity rather than another (MacAskill 2015).

The approach makes the comparison between money and time explicit. A person might be able to donate enough to fund several hours of specialist support by working for a fraction of that time. In other circumstances, direct involvement may be more valuable because the work depends on trust, experience or skills that cannot readily be purchased.

The calculation also has limits. A high salary does not automatically make a career socially beneficial, and a person's capacity to earn depends on opportunities that are unequally distributed. The source of the money matters too. A donation funded through work that causes serious harm cannot be evaluated solely by the amount subsequently given away.

In-kind giving raises another question: who decides what recipients need? After natural disasters, relief organisations often ask the public to donate money rather than send unsolicited clothes, food or household goods. Unrequested items can occupy storage space and transport capacity, while the organisations coordinating relief may have no way to distribute them appropriately. Cash allows local responders to buy supplies according to current needs and can support local markets.

The issue is not that donated goods are inherently unhelpful. Clothing or equipment may be urgently needed, and a carefully coordinated donation can meet that need. The problem arises when donors assume that their own judgement is sufficient to determine what should be sent. Elizabeth Saunders-Hastings argues that benevolent giving can become paternalistic when donors override the judgement and agency of recipients (Saunders-Hastings 2019).

Giving should therefore be evaluated partly by its effect on the person receiving it. Money, time and goods can all meet genuine needs, but none is automatically valuable simply because the giver has surrendered something. The recipient's circumstances determine much of what the contribution can achieve.

### D) Can charity address poverty and injustice, or does it leave the causes of deprivation intact?

A food bank can help a family who cannot afford groceries. It cannot, through that act alone, change the rent the family pays or the wages they receive. Charitable assistance can reduce immediate suffering while leaving the conditions that created the need largely unchanged.

Will Kymlicka argues that charity has a complicated relationship with justice. If people are deprived because social institutions distribute resources unfairly, private giving may address the consequences without correcting the arrangements responsible for them (Kymlicka 2001). In such cases, the need for charity can be evidence of a wider institutional failure.

The argument raises a problem for people who regard philanthropy as a sufficient response to inequality. Donations may help people survive under existing conditions, but they do not necessarily change the distribution of power or resources. If the same people repeatedly need assistance because their circumstances remain insecure, the success of individual donations cannot be assessed without considering the conditions in which they are made.

John Stuart Mill criticised philanthropists who addressed the consequences of unjust power while neglecting the injustice itself (Mill 1848). His point remains relevant when private donations are used to address problems that public institutions could prevent or reduce. A wealthy individual may fund emergency accommodation while opposing policies designed to improve housing security. The donation can help those in need, but it does not resolve the donor's responsibilities in other areas.

Kymlicka's account raises a further question: if justice already determines what people owe, what remains for charity to do? A duty of justice identifies claims that people may be entitled to make against others or against public institutions. Charity, by contrast, is often understood as voluntary assistance that leaves the donor discretion over whether and how to give.

Allen Buchanan challenges a rigid separation between the two. He argues that duties of aid can become sufficiently determinate to support institutional enforcement when rules establish who owes assistance and who is entitled to receive it (Buchanan 1987). On this account, the existence of a moral duty to help need not mean that every act of assistance must remain a private choice.

Zakat offers a way to consider the relationship between giving and obligation. Under Islamic law, eligible Muslims must give a prescribed share of qualifying wealth to designated categories of recipients. In Indonesia, the national zakat agency, BAZNAS, coordinates some of the collection and distribution of zakat. Unlike a donation whose amount and destination are entirely at the donor's discretion, zakat operates within a framework of defined responsibilities.

The comparison does not mean that zakat and taxation are identical. They arise from different traditions and operate under different rules. It does, however, demonstrate that giving need not be understood exclusively as a voluntary act. Some traditions treat the transfer of resources to people in need as an obligation attached to wealth itself.

Cordelli's account brings this argument back to the source of wealth. If an individual's holdings exceed what they would be entitled to under just institutions, then donating a portion may not be enough to discharge the underlying responsibility. The obligation may concern the institutions that permit the accumulation of such holdings in the first place. Taxation, labour protections and social provision can therefore form part of the response to deprivation.

Private philanthropy still has a role. Donors can support experimental research, community organisations and causes neglected by governments. Philanthropic funding can also help people organise around problems that public institutions have failed to address. Its value depends in part on whether it supplements collective provision or is used to justify withdrawing it.

The distinction matters for how wealthy donors should be judged. Someone who gives generously while using their influence to preserve arrangements that disadvantage others may be contributing to the conditions that make their philanthropy necessary. Another person might give less privately but support institutional changes that reduce the need for charitable assistance. Their contributions cannot be compared through donation totals alone.

Charity can relieve suffering and sometimes contribute to lasting change. It cannot be assumed to correct the structures that produce deprivation simply because its intentions are benevolent. The relationship between giving and justice depends on what the gift does, what it leaves untouched, and whether the people affected have any influence over the arrangements governing their lives.

### What follows from the duty to give?

The arguments examined here offer different reasons to regard giving as an obligation. Singer grounds the duty in the capacity to prevent suffering at a comparatively small cost. Kant treats beneficence as a requirement of moral life while allowing discretion over how it is practised. Cordelli questions the legitimacy of wealth accumulated under unjust institutions, while Nozick defends the claims of legitimate ownership. Their positions cannot be combined into a single rule without losing the disagreements that make them worth examining.

The amount someone should give remains contested. Carnegie offered a principle rather than a fixed sum: surplus wealth should serve the public good. Zakat specifies a rate for qualifying wealth under defined conditions, while the Giving Pledge asks participants to commit to giving away most of their fortunes. These standards use different measures and rest on different accounts of obligation. None can be applied fairly without considering the circumstances to which it refers.

A person with limited income may have little capacity to give money, while someone with substantial wealth may be able to make a large donation without compromising their own security. People can also contribute through work or care, although the value of these forms of help depends on the needs they meet. A financial total alone cannot capture every obligation, but neither should the language of generosity obscure the material difference that resources can make.

The question of giving ultimately concerns how people understand their claims over what they possess. If ownership gives someone complete discretion over their resources, charity remains a choice, however admirable. If wealth carries obligations towards people whose needs could be met with those resources, giving becomes part of what a person owes. If the wealth itself rests on unjust arrangements, the obligation may extend beyond individual donations to the institutions that govern its distribution.

Carnegie's libraries remain part of his legacy, alongside the labour dispute at Homestead. Their educational value can be acknowledged without treating philanthropy as a complete account of his responsibilities. The two aspects of his career raise questions that continue to apply to modern philanthropy: what a donation accomplishes, how the donor acquired the means to make it, and who gets to decide how those means are used.
